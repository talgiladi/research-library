## Agents in the real world: benchmarks and evaluation

### [ShellBench: even the best agents fail about four in ten everyday tasks](https://openclaw.ai/blog/shellbench/)

The Deep View's lead ([its exclusive](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/exclusive-openclaw-offers-an-agent-reality-check)) is a new benchmark from the OpenClaw Foundation, built with Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley and derived from patterns in more than 10 million anonymised agent interactions. Instead of clean puzzles, each of its 100 tasks hands the agent a short request (a median of 43 words) and a messy workspace of around 188 emails, calendar exports, spreadsheets, receipts and PDFs, usually with a complication such as stale records, look-alike accounts or a phishing instruction, and a rubric task only counts if every criterion passes. Across 53,100 graded runs of twelve models in five harnesses (Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, OpenClaw and Pi), GPT-6 Astra led at 62.5%, a hair ahead of Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.1 Sol, while harness choice barely mattered. The interesting failures are not hallucinations: in one dinner-planning task 522 of 531 attempts picked the right restaurant, yet 331 failed only because they didn't cite the files that settled the budget or timing. Reliability is shakier still, since Astra solved 75.1% of tasks at least once in three tries but only 48.8% every time, and 9.9% of otherwise fully correct runs still broke a safety rule. The full paper is still pending, so these are the foundation's own numbers ([leaderboard on Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/benchmarks/openclaw-foundation/shellbench)).

### [sudo L7: can coding agents act like staff engineers?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30517)

Passing the tests is the easy part, argue the authors of this 60-task benchmark, written by staff-level engineers mostly inside private production codebases and each built around a spot where a frontier model actually failed. Every task pairs a realistic, underspecified request with a hidden rubric of atomic criteria (1,287 in total) covering eight behaviours, from functional correctness to noticing unmentioned risks, questioning flawed premises, reusing existing code and reporting truthfully what was and wasn't checked. An agentic grader reads the transcript and runs the code, and an attempt passes only if it meets every top-severity criterion, more than half of which are about something other than whether the code works. On the November leaderboard of 19 models, Claude Fable 5.2 passes 52.2% of attempts, Sonnet 5.5 50.6% and Opus 5.5 46.1%, with GPT-6.1 Sol, Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra at 31 to 34% and everyone else below 19%. Model graders agreed with a human engineer on 85 to 86% of verdicts, about as often as two engineers agree with each other, and the case studies show how a single unsupported claim in the final report can sink an otherwise strong attempt.

### [Simon Willison: three LLM inflection points in twelve months](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/24/llm-inflection-points/)

On the first anniversary of Claude Opus 4.5, Willison expands his AI Engineer Code Summit keynote ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9RKrSF9d2SE)) into an argument that coding agents crossed three thresholds this year. The first was November 2025, when Opus 4.5 and the GPT-5.1 Codex models made Claude Code and Codex reliable enough that their default output was usually right, which is when he started using them daily. The second was June and July, when Claude Fable 5 introduced what he now calls "Fable class" models: give them a goal and the right tools and they relentlessly improvise their own instruments, as when Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 triangulated a mysterious beep to within about 40cm using five browser microphones. He counts Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra and probably Gemini 4 Neon in that class. The third, over September and October, was 3D modelling and game design: telling Opus 5.5 "make it a LOT more fun" produced 12 hours of autonomous playtesting that turned his dull RTS into something genuinely entertaining. He is still undecided whether Fable 5.2 is a fourth, but notes that a flood of Datasette and LLM releases in three weeks came from a model whose taste "almost exactly matches my own."

### [GPT-6.2 Astra identifies the artist of an anonymous 1808 sketchbook](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/24/astra-historical-mystery/)

Willison highlights a case study by history professor Mark Humphries ([original post](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gpt-62-astra-solves-a-historical)), in which GPT-6.2 Astra, with minimal prompting, worked out in 22 minutes who made an anonymous 1808 sketchbook held at Yale's Beinecke Library. It found a diary reference to a visit by the vicomte de Chabot dated five days before a date in the sketchbook at a nearby river, then predicted that Chabot's travel journal, which is only available by order from Cleveland State University, would confirm the attribution, and it did. Humphries says that journal is a significant find in its own right for Canadian and fur-trade historians. His more reflective point is that historians should expect a crisis of confidence next spring or summer, like the one mathematics is going through, but a milder one, because history is about reinterpreting the past rather than solving a single problem, and its books sit closer to philosophy and literature than to scientific papers.

### [When tools fail, agents fabricate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28341)

This study injects controlled faults into a realistic sandbox, with a real filesystem and permissions, SQLite, a local HTTP server and a constrained shell, across 8 tools, 9 fault types and 30 multi-step tasks, then checks 2,100 faulted runs from four agents against ground truth. Recovery is unreliable (19 to 68% correct) and often silent, with 6 to 26% of runs ending in a confident wrong answer stated as fact. The surprising part is that the fault type predicts fabrication better than the model does: outright denials lead to honest failure, while faults that return partial data push fabrication as high as 38%, because the agent swallows the bad data on first contact without retrying or flagging it, a pattern the authors call silent ingestion. A verification-first monitor that grounds its checks in structured tool outputs and issues corrective directives rather than hard blocks lifts correct recovery from 45.6% to 56.3% and cuts fabrication from 17.3% to 10.8%. Removing chain-of-thought makes fabrication 8.3 points worse, and the RetryRealistic toolkit and annotated traces are released.

### [Modelling how errors propagate through multi-agent LLM systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31063)

Outcome-only evaluation of multi-agent systems hides whether a pipeline amplifies, dampens or corrects a mistake made midway, so this paper proposes REPA, a structural model that measures how each agent assembles its inputs and processes them across several error channels, then predicts how errors will flow to the final answer. Tested on three Qwen base models, six multi-agent workflows and ten knowledge and reasoning settings (released as ErrPropBench-10), its predictions reach a mean Spearman correlation of 0.84 with observed system behaviour, against 0.47 for regressing on outcomes directly. The practical payoff is targeted repair: using REPA to decide where to add retrieval improves accuracy over no retrieval by an average of 19 points for only 16% more tokens.

## Agent harnesses, context and skills

### [Claude Ship: Anthropic's agents for getting pull requests merge-ready](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-ship)

TLDR and The Code both flagged Claude Ship, a trio of Anthropic agents for GitHub pull requests. Code Review hunts for logic errors, security holes and broken edge cases; PR Steward works through review comments, failing checks and merge conflicts, pushing small fixes and replying on the thread when a request is too large or is really a design question; and Claude Approvals checks each PR against a policy file the team writes, which can draw on facts such as lines changed or target branch and on a classifier's judgment of how risky a change is. The documentation is notably frank about the risks. It recommends starting PR Steward in manual mode, warns that it has no spend limit until you set one, and says Approvals should begin in an observe mode that is still billed. It also spells out how a PR can merge without any human review, for example when a policy sets zero seats for bot-authored changes or when a GitHub rule counts Claude's approval as the one required. Approvals isn't available with Zero Data Retention, HIPAA or customer-managed keys.

### [Orchestrating orchestrators: 40 parallel coding sessions in one chat room](https://www.nijho.lt/post/orchestrating-orchestrators/)

TLDR's long read is Bas Nijholt's account of how his agentic coding setup outgrew thirty terminal panes, then the Codex desktop app and T3 Code, and ended up in MindRoom, his own chat-room interface where every task is a thread and the room shows which one needs him. The turning point was an experiment of more than 4,000 short terminal runs in which giving a model its vendor's native harness tools made only one model clearly more efficient, while a minimal agent with a single shell tool and a 12-line prompt used 58 to 76% fewer tokens than his standard agent. That freed him to mix Anthropic, OpenAI and Google models in one thread. Each thread now has a single parent agent on Opus 5.5 that holds his skills, memories, review standards and git workflow and delegates edits and reviews to minimal sub-agents with short prompts, so tasks run from an hour to over a day on their own. He can run fifteen sessions comfortably and up to forty when rested, and says the real bottleneck is keeping track of all those threads in his own head.

### [Demystifying the autoresearch harness: how context and skills shape self-evolving agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28244)

Autoresearch agents that design, run and refine their own experiments usually bundle many harness tricks together, so this paper pulls them apart on MLE-Bench and KernelBench with gpt-oss-120b, GPT-6 Luna and GPT-6 Sol, comparing each choice against a simple recency-truncation baseline. On context, keeping separately accessible records of past attempts beats maintaining a rolling summary, and context folding wins in all six settings, by up to 19.2%. On skills, the counterintuitive finding is that deleting lessons that keep going unused works better on average than consolidating them or promoting them to standing rules. Combining folding with skill deletion in an agent called MLEvolver yields a 72.7% medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite and a 94% fast₁ score on KernelBench Level 3, well ahead of AIDE and OpenEvolve on the same inference budget, which makes this a useful checklist for anyone designing memory for long-horizon agents.

### [STRIDE: strategy-to-tactics progressive skill disclosure for agentic RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28907)

Most skill-augmented agents see a flat pool of skills and update them from whole-episode outcomes, which mixes up two different kinds of experience. STRIDE separates them: a chosen strategy stays visible as context for the whole episode, while tactics appear only as a compact menu whose full content loads when the agent explicitly asks for it at a local sticking point. That retrieval action becomes a unit of credit, teaching the agent when to retrieve and supplying evidence for how the skill bank should evolve. With small Qwen3 models it reaches 92.7% (4B) and 94.9% (8B) average success on ALFWorld and beats the strongest reproduced skill baseline on WebShop by 4.5 score points, which supports the progressive-disclosure pattern that today's agent skill systems already use.

### [MetaSkill: reusable problem-solving methods for orchestrating agent skills](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29274)

Having a library of skills doesn't tell an agent how to combine them, so MetaSkill adds a layer above them: it chooses one of 26 curated problem-solving methods, binds the method's abstract roles to the objects in the task, and then uses that method to set intermediate goals, coordinate native skills and decide when to repair or switch methods. On 89 SkillsBench tasks and 995 ToolHop examples it beats the baselines in all six configurations, by 3.5 to 13.5 accuracy points over the strongest, and the ablations show that explicitly orchestrating with a method beats simply injecting it into the prompt, with switching methods mid-task adding further gains.

### [SkillLens: diagnosing and refining agent skills with a human in the loop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28706)

Anyone who maintains agent skills knows the pain of working out which edit caused which change in behaviour, and SkillLens is a tool for exactly that. It links performance changes across skill versions to the individual cases that flipped, the skill diffs, the invocation sequences, the traces and the outputs, while keeping any model-generated explanation or suggested fix under human review. The design came out of a formative study with five developers, and in a 20-person between-subjects study participants found it more usable and less demanding than a baseline workflow. The contribution is modest, but the idea of versioned, case-level evidence for skill edits is easy to borrow.

### [Layer-level control of language-model execution inside agent frameworks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29519)

Agent frameworks normally treat the model as a prompt-in, text-out box, but this paper proposes a harness that exposes intermediate model states and layer-level controls to the runtime so that a policy can decide, token by token, whether to keep computing, exit early or "stitch" into another model. The prototype learns these bridges and dispatch policies from paired model states, picking the cheapest action whose full-vocabulary prediction still matches the full-depth model, and is trained on 5,000 typed-decision items built from WildChat source texts. Preliminary results on JevBench show about 15% lower mean latency at accuracy comparable to full-model inference, overhead included. It's early, but it points to harnesses that manage compute inside the model rather than only around it.

### [Post-training agents for long-document reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30090)

Retrieval benchmarks miss how hard professional documents are: in this ten-domain enterprise collection the median source runs 27 pages (up to 873), a third of tasks hinge on charts, drawings or maps, and 70.9% need two or more reasoning hops. The authors post-train the open-weight Kimi-K2.7 at its full context window on 1,465 expert-written tasks with atomic rubrics, using rank-32 adapters, one round of privileged-context self-distillation and two rounds of rubric-reward RL. Strict pass on a 100-task holdout in a 262K-token window more than doubles, from 11.0% to 24.5%, while output length stays flat at about 9,800 tokens, against 30.7% for GPT-5.6 Sol at 3,422 tokens. The more telling result is on 220 out-of-domain GDPval tasks run as a tool-using agent, where reward rises from 0.571 to 0.746 and the trained model replaces a context-thrashing retry loop with one up-front check of all its sources. The authors want long-context evaluations to report tokens and turns alongside accuracy.

### [VTree: hierarchical memory that grows toward the question for long-video agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28093)

Long videos blow past any vision-language model's context window, and current fixes either sample frames sparsely and lose the detail a question needs or re-explore from scratch for every question. VTree starts from the observation that a video's coarse structure, where scenes and topics begin and end, doesn't depend on the question, while which part to zoom into and how deep does. It therefore builds a coarse memory tree once, then for each question grows branches only where that question needs depth, stopping when the evidence suffices, and writes every expansion back so later questions reuse it. Across five long-video benchmarks it improves accuracy for several backbones while cutting context length and execution time per question, which makes it a neat example of agent memory that accumulates instead of being thrown away.

## Self-improvement and research agents

### [SELFWRITE: a model that writes its own weight edits, without gradients](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29513)

Linked from TLDR, this paper takes recursive self-improvement literally. Today's models improve through data, recipes or scaffolds, but gradient descent always writes the actual weight change. In SELFWRITE, each generation of the model studies its own reasoning on fresh problems, inspects its own weights and activations in a persistent Python session, and writes a program that builds a low-rank edit to itself from white-box primitives such as singular directions, contrasts between activations on right and wrong answers, and rank-one projections, with no training or gradient tool available. Each candidate edit is graded once on a hidden gate set, the best strictly improving one is merged, and the edited model writes the next edit. Over five generations on Qwen3.8-27B with DAPO-Math problems, gate accuracy rises from 73.95% to 76.45% and held-out test accuracy from 76.30% to 77.75% (avg@4) and from 82.60% to 84.80% (pass@4). The gains are small, but the striking claim is that the edited model keeps producing useful edits.

### [DeepMind's Gemini 4 agent teams find cheaper quantum algorithms, proven in Lean](https://deepmind.google/blog/finding-faster-quantum-algorithms-with-agents-and-formal-verification/)

Because large quantum circuits can't be run on today's hardware or simulated classically, Google DeepMind had independent teams of Gemini 4 agents search for improvements to known algorithms and prove each one correct in the Lean theorem prover before it entered a shared notebook. Every four hours the teams met at a virtual conference to trade and debate ideas, and costs fell steadily over the run. The headline is a circuit for computing a property of inertial-confinement fusion whose cost dropped 11.8x, with memory cut from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits. The agents also made the quantum simulation of a common light-sensitive molecule 8x cheaper, improved Cytochrome P450 simulation 2.5x (481 down to 333 qubits), and found a 2.57x speedup for one implementation of Grover's algorithm. The [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09822), the Lean library and the verified solutions are [open-sourced](http://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-quantum-computation).

## AI security

### [OpenAI and Cisco's Shirudo: eBPF "shields" that protect running Linux systems before the patch lands](https://openai.com/index/introducing-shirudo/)

Shirudo, Japanese for "shield", is an open-source runtime from OpenAI and Cisco for the gap between a vulnerability's disclosure and a tested patch, when blocking the vulnerable Linux operation outright could take the service down. It lets agents investigate a vulnerability, write a targeted eBPF program that blocks only the exploit path while legitimate requests pass, and validate it against both the exploit and real traffic. Operators then approve it and load it into the running system without a restart. Shields are cryptographically signed and checked by the kernel's eBPF verifier, and a TPM keeps a verifiable record of what was loaded or removed. In a controlled test ([Isovalent's write-up](https://isovalent.com/blog/post/cisco-openai-shirudo/)), a Codex-generated shield added an input check to Java's Base64 decoder inside a live Elasticsearch process and rejected a Java4Shell exploit attempt. The project is inviting outside contributors, and the results so far are the vendors' own demonstrations.

### [Who is prompt-injecting coding agents? Hidden AI-directed text on GitHub](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28763)

This is the first large-scale look at how prompt injection actually shows up on GitHub. The authors scanned roughly 145 million issues, pull requests, comments and READMEs for text that GitHub hides from human readers but agents receive in full, then filtered it with LLM classification, provenance checks and an exhaustive manual review. They confirmed 2,281 findings in issues, PRs and comments (16 per million items) and AI-directed hidden text in 107 READMEs. Outright attacks turn out to be rare, about one in 3.3 million items, and are aimed at maintainers' own triage, review and CI agents. Two-thirds of the findings are projects using hidden markers to steer their own agents, a practice that grew fivefold in four months and amounts to a control channel anyone who can comment could imitate. Replayed offline against five models, the attacks worked on one small, fast model 19% of the time and on none once HTML comments were stripped, so stripping hidden markup is a cheap defence worth having. The detector and all findings are released.

### [Carried-over and agent-native threats in the agent-skill supply chain](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30932)

Rather than hunting for malicious skills, this registry-scale study looks at accidental flaws across all 48,682 skills on ClawHub, through static screening, manual review of 3,428 flagged cases, sandbox runs and a live agent under indirect prompt injection. Dependency risk is everywhere but mostly theoretical: 49.6% of skills with resolvable dependencies pull a version with a known CVE, 78.3% of those were vulnerable from the day they were published, yet agent input reaches the vulnerable function in only 3.0% of them. First-party code is the real problem, since 21 of 22 sampled flaws were exploited end to end. The most practical finding is that a live agent executed the injected payload in 48.5% of sampled flagged skills, but almost always in its own shell before the skill even ran. The authors' advice is that registries should prioritise first-party flaws over dependency alerts, and that agent runtimes should stop passing skill arguments through a shell.

### [Tool-call smuggling: when the guard and the executor parse different calls](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30624)

An agent's tool call is a blob of JSON that gets parsed many times, by the provider, gateways, SDKs, frameworks, the safety guard and finally the executor, and JSON leaves room for disagreement, such as which copy of a repeated key counts. This paper shows that when the guard and executor disagree, the guard approves one call, the executor runs another, and the audit log records the harmless one. A differential harness across 17 pinned parsers and validators finds that most pairs disagree on some inputs, including two official MCP SDKs. On τ-bench and AgentDojo tasks, an attacker who can rewrite bytes in transit cancels another customer's reservation and creates a file the guard blocks by name, and a malicious MCP server that copies a real tool's schema and adds one documented field gets the model to fill it with user data, unseen by the guard. The fix is simple and stops all 7,412 crafted inputs: parse each call once, keep only the approved schema's fields, and seal it with its call ID on the way to the executor.

### [IntentFlow: blocking trusted-but-unauthorised tool arguments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30745)

Provenance tracking stops agents from using values that came from untrusted content, but it happily accepts a trusted value the user never asked for, such as the wrong recipient, endpoint or file. IntentFlow sits between an unmodified planner and its tools and checks every authority-bearing argument against both a provenance policy and an intent contract derived from the request. On 200 synthetic attacks, success falls from 50.5% with provenance alone to 12.0%, and all the remaining successes tamper with content or payload fields rather than with where the action goes. On 50 trusted-but-unauthorised attacks, provenance-only mediation executes all of them and IntentFlow none, while a CaMeL-style check also blocks them all but rejects 16 of 50 benign tasks. The benchmark uses oracle intent labels, so real-world intent extraction remains the open question.

### [AgentBouncer: guarding software-engineering agents at the system-call level](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30621)

Defences that inspect an agent's prompt and tool-call sequence can miss what actually happens in the environment, so AgentBouncer mediates the system calls those tool calls trigger. An LLM arbiter judges each call on two axes, how necessary it is for the current task and how safe it is, and the less necessary a call looks, the surer the arbiter has to be before allowing it. Preseeded policy rules and hierarchy-aware caching keep the arbiter out of most decisions. Against 340 attacks spanning four vectors, combined with SWE-bench Verified tasks, it cuts attack success from 99.41% for an unsupervised agent to 10.59%, 18 to 74 points better than competing defences, while failing only 0.25% of benign tool calls. The overhead is 47 seconds and $0.0036 per task, against 4.5x the time and 9.6x the cost for the closest runtime defence.

### [Preflight: agentic last-mile verification of Python dependencies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31110)

Provenance and static analysis say little about how a newly released package will behave inside your particular program, so Preflight checks dependencies against the program that uses them. It extracts the symbols the application actually references, has an agent synthesise and validate function-level test harnesses for them, runs the resolved package in a restricted environment, and compares its behavioural fingerprint with earlier versions. On four real supply-chain incidents in five application contexts it flags the malicious releases, and it correctly passes the LiteLLM client context, where the compromised proxy-server code path is never exercised. All 100 benign packages came back clean. The ablation is the convincing part: on the Ultralytics and ctx incidents, function-level harnesses exposed the malicious behaviour, while simply importing the package exposed neither.

### [Apsis: static safety analysis for agentic workflows](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28469)

Some agent failures aren't prompt injections or bad API calls at all but bugs in how the workflow is wired, such as a mandatory input that can go missing or shared state that merges concurrent writes badly. Apsis catches these before deployment by lifting framework-specific code into a common intermediate representation, a semantic graph of steps, data dependencies and shared state annotated with behavioural properties, and then matching declarative bug patterns against it. The authors built what they say is the first corpus of real workflow bugs, drawn from reports across 13 popular frameworks, and on a benchmark from it Apsis reaches 91% precision and 84% recall while pinpointing the responsible step. It also uncovered 36 previously unknown bugs in real-world workflows.

### [Do security prompts make AI-built web apps safer? A 400-app experiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28408)

This controlled experiment built each of 100 frozen app specifications four times, with Claude Code and Codex CLI, each with and without an appended block of OWASP-aligned security requirements, and then ran the same static and dynamic scans over all 400 apps, yielding 6,721 triaged findings. The security block cut findings per application by about a third, equally for both tools, and high-severity findings in first-party code fell by nearly half. The catch is that it also roughly doubled the declared dependency surface, so the security-prompted apps carried more high-severity dependency advisories, although the advisory rate per dependency did not rise. The authors trace that to library choice rather than to the requirements themselves. The takeaway is that a security prompt is a cheap, portable win, as long as you audit the extra dependencies it brings in.

### [Conartist: adaptive red-teaming that turns harness defences into attack surface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29977)

Computer-using agent harnesses rely on two defences: consistency checks that keep the agent on the user's goal, and risk audits of high-stakes operations. Conartist attacks both. Its first stage "pollutes" the goal by building plausible causal links between the user's objective and the malicious steps, so the attack looks like part of the legitimate task. Its second stage studies how the auditor responds during controlled exploration and adapts, either slipping under the audit or exploiting the extra actions the audit itself introduces. Across five backbone models it reaches 32.12% attack success on a 576-instance tool-use benchmark and 66.67% on a Reddit GUI benchmark, beating the previous best attack by 14.4 and 29.2 points. An analysis of OpenClaw yields seven realistic exploit patterns, and the uncomfortable conclusion is that security mechanisms can themselves be manipulated into helping the attacker.

### [AdaptiveVPI: web-agent defences collapse against adaptive visual prompt injection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29268)

Most defences against visual prompt injection look for injected instructions in screenshots, but a phishing destination can sit in the HTML where the screenshot never shows it, and evaluations rarely assume an attacker who knows the defence. This benchmark fixes both, with 360 webpage instances across 10 real sites and 6 hijack strategies whose malicious destinations are invisible in screenshots, plus an attack loop that refines its injection using feedback from whichever defence it faces. Against 10 defence configurations for computer-use agents and 11 for browser-use agents, the adaptive attacks average 91.87% and 91.99% success, which shows how far current defences are from realistic threats.

### [Persuasion from within: learned insider attacks on multi-agent debate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28660)

Multi-agent debate and consensus setups assume every participant shares the group's goal, and this paper shows what a single disguised insider can do. The attacker first learns a world model of the benign agents from offline trajectories, then uses it both to train a persuasion policy and to plan its messages in real time. In a numerical consensus game and a multi-agent debate, it is both the most effective and the stealthiest attack tested, beating prompt-based and trained LLM persuaders, and it transfers to other target models, larger groups and unseen datasets while surviving existing Byzantine-resilience defences. The good news is that the attack data doubles as a defence: adversarially training the benign agents on it largely neutralises both this attack and the strongest earlier one.

### [Adaptation or poisoning? Jailbreaking test-time-learning chat models through their own updates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29532)

Test-time learning lets a deployed model adapt by updating a lightweight adapter on each input it sees, and if those updates persist across turns, every user query is also training data. P-TAP exploits that with nothing but ordinary queries: an escalating sequence shapes the session adapter turn by turn, and the final harmful request, which is never itself used for adaptation, is answered under the poisoned state. Across four open-weight models, enabling updates raises attack success by 13 points with greedy decoding and 22 points with best-of-10 sampling compared with identical sessions without updates. Screening incoming queries flags every attack stream before the final request, but a perplexity-based detector of unsafe adaptation catches none of the successful attacks, a warning for anyone shipping persistent per-user adaptation.

### [PJBack: a jailbreak backdoor that survives safety alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29217)

Jailbreak backdoors, where a secret trigger phrase makes a model comply with harmful prompts, usually behave so differently from the refusal training data that later safety alignment washes them out. PJBack narrows that gap by minimising the divergence between clean and triggered continuations during decoding, so poisoned samples look like ordinary safety samples, and adds a deferred-response mechanism that delays the harmful content to disguise it further. Across eight models from five families, from 7B to 70B parameters, it averages 93.9% attack success, bypasses all seven defences tested and keeps benign utility intact, while five earlier backdoor attacks either succeed less often or fail to survive the defences. That raises the stakes for anyone fine-tuning on third-party data or open checkpoints.

### [Relative robustness: a formal security property for prompt injection and enclaves](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29802)

This formal-methods paper extends "relative security", which judges a system against a reference system and has only covered confidentiality, with relative integrity and a stronger property, relative robustness, which requires the same reference runs to reproduce both what leaks and what effects an attack causes. For LLM agents under prompt injection, the reference runs the same plan with a model that never obeys untrusted content, and the same machinery applies to C programs split across SGX or TrustZone enclaves. The point is that some attacks pass the weaker checks and fail only the stronger one, including an injection that makes a support-desk model accept any customer, as one Tensor Trust input did, and a step of the Secret Network attack. Everything is mechanised in Isabelle/HOL. It's theory-heavy, but it gives a precise way to say what an injection defence has actually guaranteed.
