## Newsletters

### [Sharing AI progress in mathematics](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/)
OpenAI dumped 722 manuscripts covering 372 result families onto GitHub, all produced by an unreleased internal model that was posed roughly 4,000 open problems after the company’s existing math evals saturated. The headline claim is a “quasi-Riemann hypothesis,” a proof that every Dirichlet L-function, zeta included, has no zeros beyond Re s > 7/8. Most results came from the same fixed procedure at about three hours of ChatGPT Pro-equivalent compute each; 162 papers have a Lean-formalized main result, and OpenAI concedes some unformalized ones “could have issues.” The reception is the other half of the story. WIRED reports that mathematicians had asked OpenAI not to release everything at once and quotes one professor describing a “perception of mobster behavior,” while an open letter signed by 31 Fields medalists, Terence Tao and Peter Scholze among them, warns that mass-produced true/false statements could wreck the slow human process that turns results into understanding.

### [Claude now works in Google Docs, Sheets, and Slides](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-now-works-in-google-docs-sheets-and-slides)
Anthropic put Claude for Google Workspace into public beta on every paid plan. It adds a sidebar that reads the open doc, sheet or deck, sees what you have selected and edits in place: in Docs it can restyle a heading without disturbing the surrounding formatting or offer larger rewrites as suggestion cards, in Sheets it writes formulas, pivot tables and native charts and can pull a range into Python for joins and cleanup, and in Slides it builds new slides from the deck’s own layouts and then checks for overlaps or unreadable text. By default every change appears as an approval card, though an “Accept all edits” mode lets it work through a task unattended. More important for teams, the sidebar brings your existing connectors and skills along, so a QBR deck can draw on Salesforce history and Drive call notes, while enterprise controls such as the Compliance API, CMEK and OpenTelemetry audit export still apply. Separate Docs, Sheets and Slides connectors let Claude create and edit Google files from the chat side too.

### [Expanding the Cyber Verification Program](https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)
Anthropic is folding Project Glasswing and its earlier Cyber Verification Program into a single three-tier offer that loosens cyber safeguards for vetted defenders. Defense Access covers SOC work, malware reverse engineering and vulnerability validation, and is open even to small hospitals, utilities, open-source maintainers and individual researchers, with decisions promised in days. Red Team Access adds authorized penetration testing but keeps real-time blocks on ransomware and physical damage, while Specialized Access, the least restricted tier, is reserved for organizations testing grids, flight systems and interbank rails and is reviewed with the US government. The interesting part is that Anthropic published how well the tiers actually gate capability: on CyScenarioBench, Opus 5.5 was blocked on every task’s first prompt without CVP, blocked in 46 of 50 runs under Defense Access, and completed 34 of 50 under Red Team Access, matching its unguarded rate. Glasswing partners reported more than 129,000 verified vulnerabilities between April and July, and data retention is mandatory for monitoring until Enterprise Frontier Safeguards arrive later this fall.

### [The Cyber Risk Discourse is Broken](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/the-cyber-risk-discourse-is-broken)
Nathan Lambert argues that the push to restrict open-weight models on cyber grounds is heading for a lose-lose policy, because it treats a ban as if it happened in a vacuum. Taking aim at Anthropic’s recent report on GLM-5.3 as an offensive tool, he notes that the public evidence so far ties most documented AI-enabled attacks to closed models served through APIs, which suggests “open unsafe, closed unsafe” is closer to the truth than “open dangerous, closed safe.” If open models must be banned to slow the spread of cyber capability, he reasons, frontier APIs would logically have to go too, and banning only open weights would widen the offense-defense gap, since air-gapped agencies and defenders running on their own infrastructure depend on them. He closes with a pointed claim: an accidental open release of Claude Mythos would have looked more like an acceleration of existing threats than a step change, a prediction the next few months will put to the test.

### [How to fix a bug in a fix](https://projectzero.google/2026/10/emergency-patching.html)
Google Project Zero has written a vendor playbook for the scenario LLMs make more likely, in which many more attackers find and exploit bugs faster than normal release trains can ship fixes. Its central observation is that emergency patches are rarely slowed by triage or writing the fix; testing and delivery are the real bottlenecks, so the answer is to build fast lanes ahead of time. The post walks through four of them. Feature flags can switch off a vulnerable path, as Apple did with Group FaceTime in 2019 and as Meta’s “dual stack” WebRTC build allows. Dynamically updated filters such as Android’s Intent Firewall block exploit inputs, alternate channels like Android’s APEX ship component updates outside full OS releases, and hotpatching swaps functions in running memory. Each comes with a security cost, especially side-loaded libraries that lack proper signing checks, but the message is blunt: decide how you would fix an actively exploited bug before you have one.

### [Introducing Personal Agent Protocol](https://sierra.ai/blog/introducing-personal-agent-protocol)
Sierra and Meta, joined by Genesys, Instinct, Rocket, Shopify, Stripe and Walmart, are drafting an open standard for how personal agents deal with businesses, so they no longer have to click through websites or wait on support lines. An agent finds out what a company offers on its site, starts a session as a guest or signs in with credentials the customer has delegated, and the user chooses between read-only and write access. The session runs on OAuth and carries across channels, so a question asked before sign-in and an order change made afterward belong to the same visit, while the company decides whether the agent works through its web pages, MCP or OpenAPI interfaces, or its own service agent. Bret Taylor told CNBC it is “kind of chaos until such a standard exists,” with Meta’s Muse topping the App Store even as Amazon blocks its agents over scraping concerns. A v0.1 spec and reference implementation are due later this month, with finer-grained permissions, push notifications and card-free payments on the roadmap.

### [The Code Nobody Reads](https://addyo.substack.com/p/the-code-nobody-reads)
Addy Osmani now says the opposite of his old advice to review every line of AI-generated code. Line-by-line reading is going away, in his view, but review in the sense of someone owning the decision to ship is not, and the teams in trouble are the ones that stopped reading diffs without building anything to replace them. He anchors the argument in Anthropic’s own numbers. Its multi-agent Code Review system, which runs on nearly every internal PR, raised the share of PRs getting substantive comments from 16% to 54%, sees fewer than 1% of findings marked wrong, and in a retrospective would have caught about a third of the bugs behind past claude.ai incidents. What machines don’t replace is what review mostly did: a 2013 Microsoft study found only 14% of comments were about defects, with the rest teaching juniors and passing on team norms. His formula is agents for the first pass and humans for blast radius, with accountability that comes with real authority over pace.

### [Claude Code in the cloud: a field guide to cloud sessions](https://claude.dev/blog/claude-code-in-the-cloud/)
In a second piece the same week, Osmani walks through Claude Code cloud sessions using four real runs. Each task gets a fresh VM with the repo cloned onto its own branch, so `claude --cloud` jobs can run side by side without fighting over files or ports, and they keep going after the laptop goes to sleep. In his demo, three sessions launched together were all done within 87 seconds: one traced a flaky test to a race in a cache loader and ran the suite 40 times clean, another rebuilt stale API docs by curling every endpoint, and the third spotted the same race from its own branch but left it alone as out of scope. The security design is the part worth noting: your GitHub token never enters the VM, because a proxy hands the session a short-lived credential that can push only to its own branch. The repo’s CLAUDE.md and skills come along while your personal `~/.claude` stays home, which makes a cloud session a cleaner sandbox than a local agent holding your SSH keys and cloud CLIs.

### [EmbeddingGemma 2: an open, lightweight multimodal embedding model](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/embeddinggemma-2/)
Google’s second EmbeddingGemma extends the on-device embedder, downloaded more than 20 million times, from text to code, images, video and audio, all mapped into one shared 768-dimensional space. Built on Gemma 4 and released under Apache 2.0, it is modular: a 270M text-and-code core plus optional vision (+170M) and audio (+300M) encoders, so the full model comes to 740M parameters and runs in about 567MB of RAM on a quantized Pixel 11 Pro. The context window grows to 8K tokens, enough for about 5.5 minutes of audio or 58 video frames, and Matryoshka truncation down to 128 dimensions cuts vector storage by up to six times. For agent builders, the most notable number is code retrieval, which jumps from 68.76 to 78.68 on MTEB Code, and Google’s developer guide demonstrates exactly that by indexing the transformers codebase for a Gemma 4 agent running in the Pi harness. The Deep View pairs it with Reflection’s Beam as evidence that US open models are finally gaining ground on China’s.

### [Nano Banana 2.1 – Model Card](https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/nano-banana-2-1/)

### [How Netflix Taught an LLM to Recommend Movies So That You Keep Watching](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/how-uber-built-a-genie-to-answer)
ByteByteGo unpacks GenRec, Netflix’s attempt to replace thousands of hand-built ranking features with an LLM that reads a plain-text “verbalization” of a member’s history and context. The model is trained in two stages: an open foundation model is first adapted on Netflix data into a slow-changing domain base, then frequently post-trained to rank, using engagement logs rewritten as conversations and reward models that favor return visits and exploration over binge-driven repetition. The interesting part is how much of the work turns out to be context engineering. Weak signals like hovers are dropped, repeated episodes are compressed into a single event, cold-start titles get richer metadata, and the token count falls to about a third, with serving cost falling in line. GenRec never writes out a list. A ranking head scores catalog embeddings against a pooled hidden state in a single prefill-only pass, so it cannot invent titles, and shared prompt prefixes make caching cheap. A four-week A/B test on about 10% of traffic produced small but statistically significant gains on both short-term and long-term metrics.

## Agent memory

### [AgentMemGate: Addressing Speculation Contamination in Conversational Assistant Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07707)

Tell an assistant you might move to Seattle and there is a fair chance its long-term memory will record that you already live there. The authors call this speculation contamination, and final-state memory benchmarks rarely catch it. On a new set of multi-session conversations where plans are later confirmed, abandoned or left hanging, Mem0 and Graphiti store unresolved plans as current fact for 35.2% and 27.3% of pending plans. AgentMemGate is a write-time gate that sorts extracted statements into speculation, completed event, correction or other, and keeps speculation out of the profile store until it is confirmed. On the core benchmark it removes all observed contamination and lifts task accuracy from 65% to 95%. The remaining weak spot is mundane: many realistic plans match no profile field and never reach the gate.

### [ReFold: Training-Free Reversible Inter-Turn Context Folding for Long-Horizon Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07863)

Most context-management schemes for long agent runs decide what to throw away, which costs extra model calls, breaks prefix caches and loses content for good. ReFold leaves the underlying history untouched and only compresses what gets rendered to the model: content an earlier turn already displayed becomes a stub, and turns the agent itself has marked as finished fold into a one-line note. Because every removal is reversible, a bad fold costs one restore instead of a lost fact, and rewriting only every few steps keeps prefix caching intact. Dropped into standard ReAct-style harnesses across five long-horizon benchmarks and two frontier models, it cuts tokens by up to 2.5x and halves per-session KV-cache memory without hurting task success. Under capped budgets it also avoids up to 92% of forced compactions.

### [Stateless Language Agents: Scaling Long-Horizon Automated Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07625)

Give automated research agents a billion-token budget and they tend to waste it, replaying ever-longer histories, duplicating each other’s work or quietly stopping experimenting while the meter runs. This paper traces both problems to where research state lives and who chooses the next experiment, and its answer is “stateful search with stateless agents.” No agent carries its conversation between calls; instead the harness owns the candidate solutions and measured results, builds a fresh role-specific context for each call, and a lightweight stateless Advisor hands concrete experiments to parallel Workers. Against three recent frameworks on software engineering, kernel optimization and algorithm design, SLA posts the best final result on every task and matches the strongest kernel baseline with more than 84% fewer tokens.

### [DAEDALUS: Bootstrapping Agent Memory from Self-Generated Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08048)

Agents dropped into an unfamiliar environment keep rediscovering the same tool quirks, and the usual fixes, human-written guidelines or procedural memory distilled from training tasks with an oracle, assume someone already knows the environment. DAEDALUS gets there through self-play: an explorer agent invents tasks that are hard but solvable, a solver attempts them, and each failure yields a candidate heuristic that enters the memory bank only after the solver repeatedly succeeds with it in context. Across AppWorld, τ²-bench and AutomationBench, the resulting memory lifts mean success by up to 15.9 points and pass^5 by up to 2.2x over a no-memory agent, competitive with methods that rely on training tasks. The heuristics also transfer to other model families, and the self-generated tasks even rank models much like the real benchmarks do.

### [When Old Facts Return: Re-Reads, Reverts, and the Limits of Temporal Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07715)

A temporal memory can retire an obsolete value and then quietly bring it back simply because the old statement shows up again, say, when an agent re-reads a stale file. The trouble is that a re-read and a genuine revert produce exactly the same sequence of observed values while demanding opposite answers. On 130 atomic transitions drawn from real software fixes, identity-based temporal memory scores 98.5% in the ordinary case, but appending one verbatim re-read of the old statement drops accuracy to 10.8% and pushes the stale-value rate to 88.5%. A guard that refuses to reactivate retired values restores 97.7%, yet it then cannot recognize a legitimate revert without extra change provenance. The design lesson is crisp: memory systems need to tell an observation of a value apart from evidence that the value actually changed.

### [Persistent Memory in Multi-Agent LLM Inference: What It Costs, What It Buys, and When You Can Tell](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07782)

Many multi-agent systems bolt on a persistent tier that stores and recalls reasoning traces, then justify it with an ablation showing an accuracy bump. This paper measures one three-tier architecture carefully and finds that the decomposition half works, cutting the peak KV working set to 14.3 MiB per query against roughly 35 MiB for single-pass and RAG baselines, while the persistent tier adds cache cost and no detectable accuracy change across eight controlled dataset pairs. The authors argue the null is structural: single-question benchmarks hand each item its own evidence, leaving recall nothing useful to find. The more unsettling part is that reaching the null took four measurement corrections, none of them visible in the results table, so the paper closes with conditions any agent-memory ablation should meet.

## Agent harness

### [HarnessSecurity-Bench: Do Security Mechanisms Really Protect Coding Agent Harnesses?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07639)

Coding-agent harnesses are where tool use actually gets authorized, so this study asks how much their security settings really buy. The authors build a ten-mechanism taxonomy and rate 400 harness-mechanism combinations, finding that about half of confirmed mechanisms are opt-in and that closed-source harnesses leave big evidence gaps. Their benchmark then runs 23 attack tasks across Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, gptme, Qwen Code and GitHub Copilot on a fixed model, for 2,500 trials and more than 81,000 tool calls. The headline number is stark: turning on auto-approve raises utility but pushes attack success from 29.2% to 95.6%. Network isolation and read-only mode cut attacks at a steep utility cost, while allow- and denylists come cheaper, though allowed tools can leave the protected operation reachable through another execution path.

### [Catching Developers in the Flow: Low-Latency Agentic Program Repair at Google Scale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07289)

Most automated program repair runs offline after code is submitted, long after the developer has moved on. FlowAgent is Google’s attempt to fix failing tests in the pre-submit loop while the author is still in context, wired into the internal Critique and Cider tools with a ReAct-style generate-and-validate loop plus strict abstention filters before and after execution to keep suggestions fast and trustworthy. On a hand-checked set of 195 real test failures it proposed a correct fix 67.18% of the time. More telling is the deployment data: since rolling out company-wide it has suggested fixes on 295,508 changes, developers previewed 65,069 of them and applied 28,554. Developer interviews suggest engineers welcome an autonomous repair agent inside their everyday tools, even if plenty of open challenges remain.

### [Verifying Coordination in Parallel Coding Agents: NP-Bench and a Scheduling Planner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07261)

Run several coding agents on one codebase and each can pass its own tests while the merged result is broken, because two of them rewrote the same function or one coded against a contract a teammate just changed. Most coordination tools detect the collision and warn, which at agent speed arrives after the wasted edit. This paper treats coordination as an up-front scheduling problem: partition the work into disjoint declared scopes and order merges along the producer-to-consumer graph before anyone starts. Measured on NP-Bench, which verifies integration with a real git merge, the planner lifts clean integration from 1 of 9 scenarios to 9 of 9 and cuts merge conflicts from 13 to zero. Notably, the advantage did not shrink with stronger models, since it comes from how work is allocated.

### [MemMux: Runtime Verification and Honest Resource Attribution for Fleets of Parallel Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07257)

Developers increasingly run ten coding agents side by side on one machine, each spawning language servers, test runners and browsers, yet tmux and the new agent managers were built to arrange windows, not to govern memory. MemMux treats that as a runtime-verification problem, emitting checkable signals for per-agent memory attribution, complete cleanup after an agent dies, escaped child processes and a bounded footprint under overcommit. Under a 7.5 GiB budget it keeps the fleet in bounds with zero swap by admitting a subset of agents and reclaiming under pressure, while ungoverned tools pin the machine at twice the budget and spill about 2 GiB into swap. It also reclaims a terminated agent’s entire process tree where a raw baseline strands half, at a candidly reported cost of 2.7% CPU for monitoring ten agents.

### [Agent in a Bottle: Can LLM Agents Turn Their Capabilities Into Cheap, Scalable Artifacts?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08775)

Calling a frontier model millions of times for a narrow task gets expensive, so this benchmark asks whether agents can “bottle” their own abilities into something cheaper, such as a small trained model or a reusable program. In BOTTLED, agents get an entire unlabelled workload plus fixed time, compute and API budgets and choose their own approach. The results are humbling: 48 of 60 bottling runs land below their model’s zero-shot performance, and 31 underperform a simple small-model distillation baseline with the same token budget, so strong zero-shot ability does not predict good bottling. When it works, though, the savings are dramatic. On query-product relevance, Opus 5 keeps about 82% of its zero-shot macro-F1 at roughly 657 times lower cost, and recovers about 94% of the purpose-built Jev model’s score at a quarter of its projected cost.

### [One Step at a Time: Trading LLM Autonomy for Process Predictability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07817)

Companies that hand a standard operating procedure to an agent usually paste it into the system prompt and get only a final answer back, with no reliable way to know which process actually ran. This paper instead delivers the procedure over the Model Context Protocol one step at a time: an MCP server releases a step, the agent executes it and returns a structured step output, and the completed records become an auditable execution log. Across 15,475 trials on 13 SOP-Bench domains with executors ranging from Kimi K2.5 down to Ministral 3 8B, process adherence rises from 76–95% to 95–99%, and ungrounded answers nearly disappear. That matters, since under prompt-based delivery up to 49% of correct answers on one domain skipped the SOP entirely. Small models even gain accuracy, while capable ones trade a little for auditability.

## LLM security

### [APEX: Active Protection at Execution Boundaries for LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06966)

As agents stitch together tools, MCP servers and skills, the number of places an indirect prompt injection can hide keeps growing, and defenses that learn attack patterns keep falling behind. APEX stops chasing carriers and guards the one point every attack must cross, the execution boundary where internal state becomes an external action or released output. Before any untrusted content runs, it compiles an authorization contract from the trusted task, then admits an effect only when that contract justifies it, while a deception layer makes unendorsed use of runtime data expose itself before the action commits, with no taint tracking or attack-specific policies. Against 13 baselines it reaches 0% attack success on five of six benchmarks and 0.56% on the sixth, and holds at zero under adaptive attacks.

### [SkillPoison: Progressive Skill Poisoning via Successful Experiences](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07645)

Self-improving agents that distill successful runs into reusable skills open a subtler attack than planting malicious instructions. SkillPoison never makes any single trajectory malicious: it feeds the skill extractor a set of genuinely successful experiences that reinforce a target behavior, then strips away the contextual conditions that limit when that behavior should apply. The extractor generalizes the way the attacker intends, producing a skill that helps on the tasks it was learned from and does harm when it is applied somewhere it should not be. Across three benchmarks the attack reaches 95.71% success while every injected experience remains task-correct and passes verification and lexical inspection, which is exactly what makes it worrying for any pipeline that trusts “it worked” as a filter for what goes into memory.

### [Surviving the Router: Optimizing Skill Injections for Retrieval and Execution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08098)

Studies of malicious third-party agent skills usually assume the poisoned skill has already been picked, but in a realistic setup it first has to beat many legitimate skills in a router’s retrieval step. Accounting for that competition cuts the effective success of existing skill injections by 87–97%, which suggests earlier threat estimates were inflated. The bad news is that attackers can close the gap. CORSA is a router-aware attack that optimizes an injected skill first to get retrieved across clusters of related tasks and then to succeed end to end, while keeping the skill natural-looking and still useful to the user. Evaluated on an extended SkillRouter benchmark with eight payload categories, it substantially improves both retrieval and end-to-end attack success, and the optimized skills transfer across router architectures and LLM backbones.

### [The Model Plants the Trigger: Answer-Side Backdoor Attacks in Multi-Turn Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07723)

Almost every LLM backdoor hides its trigger in the user’s input, so guardrails are built to sanitize the input side. This attack flips that assumption. A benign first-turn prompt nudges a poisoned model into producing a specific, innocuous-looking word, and once that word sits in the dialogue history it becomes the trigger, so when a harmful request arrives later the model recognizes its own planted cue and skips its refusal while the user’s text stays perfectly clean. Across four LLMs the attack approaches 100% success at just a 5% poisoning rate, keeps general utility and clean-input safety intact, and slips past mainstream input-centric defenses. Representation analysis shows the self-generated trigger consistently suppresses the model’s refusal signal, a blind spot for any defense that never looks at what the model itself said.

### [Understanding and Enhancing Backdoor Persistency in LLM Agent Post-Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07510)

Teams that build software-engineering agents by fine-tuning a third-party model may assume their own benign training washes out anything nasty hidden inside. This supply-chain study finds that supervised fine-tuning does erode a planted backdoor substantially, but the reinforcement-learning stage that usually follows often preserves what is left and sometimes even strengthens it. Tracing why some backdoors survive points to initial strength and how compatible their gradients are with benign training, and the authors turn that into PersistBD, a pre-release refinement that makes a backdoor sturdier. On Qwen2.5-Coder-7B it raises attack success after SFT from 20% to 74%, and after SFT plus RL from 20% to 76%, with no visible drop in normal task performance. The takeaway for agent builders is that inherited models need active backdoor screening.

### [AdvSim2Real: Training Web Agents Against Adaptive Prompt Injection in a Web World Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08773)

Web agents cannot just ignore page content, since the page holds the values and buttons the task needs, which is why planted instructions work so well against them. Defenses fine-tuned on fixed injections fall to attackers who adapt. AdvSim2Real co-evolves three things inside a frozen web world model: a task curriculum rewarded for tasks the agent solves about half the time, an injection adversary rewarded only when it flips a success into a failure, and the agent itself. Training entirely in simulation makes a 4B agent both more capable and more robust. Across 150 web tasks, completion under an unseen frontier-model adversary improves by 33.6% relative to the base agent, and the gains carry over to a real browser.

### [Quoting Jake Boggan](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/7/jake-boggan/)

OpenAI’s openai/math repository collects 722 manuscripts produced by an unreleased internal model, and one entry claims a Lean-formalized proof of Barnette’s conjecture, the decades-old question of whether every 3-connected cubic bipartite planar graph has a Hamiltonian cycle. Willison picked out a Hacker News comment from Jake Boggan, who spent 24 years on and off working on that very problem and describes the news of its apparent solution as sad “in a far-off way, like hearing an ex-girlfriend died suddenly in a car crash.” It is a rare human-scale reaction to AI-generated mathematics, and Boggan suspects plenty of people were feeling the same odd mix of emotions that night.
