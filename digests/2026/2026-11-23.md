## Agent harnesses and evals

### [How devs are using decision models (The Code, Sunday Special)](https://codenewsletter.ai/p/how-devs-are-using-decision-models)

Two months after TypeSafe went viral with Jev, a model that can't write a single word, OpenAI, Microsoft, Cloudflare, AWS and Google have all shipped their own "decision models," and The Code's Sunday edition turns the trend into three workflows you can try. A decision model answers a closed question about whatever you hand it, such as a diff, a log line or an agent trace. It returns a yes/no, a choice or a score with a probability, so your code can act above a cutoff and hand anything below it to an LLM or a person. The newsletter warns that not every model's scores are honest enough to trust without testing. The first workflow is a [Vercel engineer's script](https://vercel.com/i/triage-github-pull-requests-openai-decisions-api) that asks OpenAI's Decisions API what kind of review a PR needs and whether existing callers will break, which targets the ten-line "cleanup" diffs that get approved in thirty seconds. The second is Amazon's [swap of Claude Sonnet for Strands Decider](https://strandsagents.com/blog/amazon-saves-millions-replacing-sonnet-with-strands-decider/), a small AWS-built model that labels tickets in one of its largest internal queues. The swap cut cost per ticket by 98% and returned answers almost five times faster, and the team ran both models side by side on live traffic with a 45-second switch back. The third, and the most useful, is [Hamel Husain and Antaripa Saha's eval study](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/decision-model-evals/). They scored six decision models on 100 labelled traces from an apartment-leasing agent and found that tuning each model's threshold, rather than trusting the default 0.5, matters a lot. Only Perplexity's Decider, Liquid's d1-ultra and OpenAI Decisions reached 80% precision and recall. OpenAI scored best (F1 0.87) at about 4.5 times Perplexity's cost (F1 0.79, 0.82 cents per 100 runs), and the post includes copy-paste prompts so a coding agent can tune thresholds and check calibration for you. The cost and speed figures come from the vendors and authors and haven't been independently checked.

### [Not everything needs an LLM: PostHog on decision models in production](https://posthog.com/newsletter/decision-models)

The Code links to PostHog's account of putting decision models like Jev into production, and it reads like a field manual for when to use them and when not to. The best example is a privacy gate in Replay Vision, PostHog's session-summary product. Each summary gets two narrow yes/no questions, whether it names a real person and whether it shows contact details or an ID, which cut false flags to about a tenth of what one broad question produced. Gemini rewrites a summary only if either answer scores 0.4 or higher, and in production 98.7% of summaries skip the LLM call. The threshold is low on purpose, because a missed leak costs more than an extra rewrite. A Slack bot that answers untagged questions takes the opposite approach and requires 0.85 so it doesn't get annoying, and the same bot uses one choice question as a model router to "pick the cheapest option that will most likely finish the task correctly on the first try." The warnings are just as practical. Thresholds shifted between v1 and v2 of the same model, chaining questions into multi-step judgments flooded the model server for hours, and ranking PR-review findings with Jev came out at chance level (AUC 0.52). That last failure is why PostHog now defaults to Jeeves, its own hybrid model that reasons before deciding when it's unsure. The cost claim of up to 444.6x cheaper and 193.6x faster than frontier models comes from TypeSafe.
