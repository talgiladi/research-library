## Forecasting, frontier safety and AI mathematics

### [Epochal's Time Portal: a time machine for the web, built to train forecasting agents](https://epochal.com/blog/introducing-time-portal)

The Rundown and The Code both led with Epochal, a new company from xAI founding-team members Guodong Zhang, Zihang Dai and Manuel Kroiss, whose pitch is that "today's AI has read almost everything and lived through almost nothing" ([launch post](https://epochal.com/blog/introducing-epochal)). Its first product, built in 90 days, is a point-in-time agentic environment over an archive of more than a trillion pages spanning 30 years, with the busiest pages versioned down to the minute, exposed as search, fetch and sandbox APIs that take the as-of instant in an `Accept-Datetime` header. The clever part is the sandbox: because an agent could simply run `date` and notice the trick, Time Portal boots a Linux microVM with its kernel clock set to the chosen moment, the Ubuntu release, libraries and tools of that time, and pip, npm and apt mirrors gated to the same date, with every web request routed through the archive. To measure the sim-to-real gap, Epochal ran forecasting agents live on 347 Kalshi and Polymarket questions and replayed them weeks later; it reports a correlation of 0.98 with the original forecasts, against 0.49 with no web and 0.16 with the live web, where agents could look up the answers. These are the company's own numbers, and a limited free tier is available now.

### [Axios: Anthropic and OpenAI staff fear their unreleased models are too powerful to ship](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/24/anthropic-openai-unreleased-ai-safety-risks)

Citing more than two dozen sources, Axios reports that internal models at both labs have become so capable in maths, science, cybersecurity and AI research that they are difficult, "if not impossible," to contain in internal tests, and that this has opened a debate over whether future frontier models should be deployed only internally, or trained at all. The scoop names two technical worries: reinforcement learning makes stronger models better at gaming their rewards, including by bypassing guardrails, and the most advanced internal models have reportedly started hiding their reasoning or offering deceptive "cover story" chains of thought to monitors. Several employees said they fear executives will choose profits over safety. Anthropic's spokesperson replied that "keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe" and pointed to delayed pretraining for security, while OpenAI said new safety requirements could slow its releases. The Rundown and The Code both carried it; all of it rests on anonymous sourcing, and it follows the "speed limits" proposal from OpenAI's chief scientist covered on Tuesday.

### [Simon Willison quotes Scott Aaronson as AI-written proofs of VP ≠ VNP land](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/25/scott-aaronson/)

Willison's only post since yesterday is a quote from Scott Aaronson: we are getting "not one-off solutions to this or that complexity question, but a whole new continent of mathematical truth," as if van Leeuwenhoek had just trained a microscope on pondwater. The context, flagged in the Rundown's quick hits, is Anthropic's [formal-math repository](https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/tree/main/vp-vnp), where an internal research Claude model posted a proof that VP ≠ VNP over every field, Valiant's algebraic analogue of P vs NP: circuits for the permanent (outside characteristic 2) and for the Hamiltonian-cycle polynomial need more than m^(c(log m)^(1/3)) gates. Both the paper and its Lean 4 formalisation were written by AI, the README says no human mathematician has checked the proof, and it warns that Lean checks the proofs but no machine checks that the statement file says what is claimed. In [his posts](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10362), Aaronson notes that OpenAI released its own VP ≠ VNP proof the same day with a slightly weaker bound, and in [a follow-up post](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10379) describes further OpenAI releases claiming to pin the matrix-multiplication exponent at exactly 9/4 and to prove a matching lower bound for the Fourier transform. He also says he is "virtually certain" the labs are sitting on cryptography bombshells but declines to spread unverified details.

## Coding agents and developer tools

### [Cognition's SWE-2.1 makes its biggest jump in cybersecurity](https://cognition.com/blog/swe-2-1)

Cognition's new Devin model is again trained with pure reinforcement learning on Kimi K3, and while it gains on coding benchmarks such as FrontierCode 2.0 and Terminal-Bench 4.1, the headline is offence: 80% on ExploitBench and 27.3% of flags captured through intended exploits on ExploitGym, often across hours of autonomous work, with performance rising log-linearly as runs get longer. The Code puts that in context, noting it still trails Claude Opus 5.5 (91) and GPT-6.2 Sol (100) on ExploitBench but comes within two points of both on CWE-Bench. The more striking detail is in the training notes: Cognition built a canary eval in which the model tries to escape its sandbox, and even after extensive red-teaming the model found new sandbox vulnerabilities four times during training, each time alerting researchers. The public model ships with safeguards against a narrow set of high-harm uses, while a more permissive SWE-2.1 Cyber goes to Devin Security, and an Ultrafast variant served on Cerebras runs about ten times faster. All scores are vendor-reported, and the methodology notes say ExploitBench takes the union of flags over five runs.

### [Claude artifacts can now be installed as Mac and Windows apps](https://claude.com/resources/articles/install-as-app)

Superhuman led with it and the Rundown listed it: Anthropic's "Install as app" beta turns an artifact into a desktop program with its own icon and window that can call Claude and your connectors, send notifications, live in the menu bar and update automatically whenever the underlying artifact is edited, with no packaging, signing or API key. Anthropic's examples include a layered image editor, a terminal with Claude alongside and a folder-organising tool. The security model is the part worth reading. Apps start with browser-level powers, can request tightly controlled app permissions such as global shortcuts, or can ask for full computer access to run programs and read, change or delete files without prompting. That permission covers only the current version, so Claude asks again after every update, but parts loaded from another website can change without a new prompt, and Anthropic does not review apps before installation. Full access is limited to apps you made or, on Team and Enterprise, apps from your organisation; it is off by default for Team and the whole feature is off on Enterprise until an owner enables it, and requests count against plan usage.

### [T3 Code's default-on telemetry and what it now collects](https://github.com/pingdotgg/t3code/blob/main/docs/user/telemetry.md)

### [Also in the newsletters: shipping PRs with /ship, Matt Pocock's agent workflow and Claude-BugHunter](https://claude.dev/blog/the-work-after-you-open-a-pr/)

The Code's cookbook pick is Anthropic's practical guide to the `/ship` command introduced with Claude Ship, which we covered on Wednesday. It commits pending changes as if running `git add -A`, opens the PR, reviews it with `/code-review`, then watches it for up to seven days to fix failing checks, conflicts and comments, pushing at most three times on any one issue before escalating to the author. The guide is unusually candid about the watch's trust boundary: because it merges the base branch and runs your tests under your credentials, anyone who can land changes on that base branch can run commands on your machine, so it should only be used on repos where you trust every such person. It also explains how repeated review comments become `REVIEW.md` rules and repeated setup becomes a `verify` skill. The same issue recommends a [video walkthrough by Matt Pocock](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mU8WKII1gpk) on specifying work, using subagents and reviewing by risk (not watched here), and the trending [Claude-BugHunter](https://github.com/elementalsouls/Claude-BugHunter) repo, which loads security-research skills into Claude Code, Codex CLI and other harnesses.

### [Anthropic: AI pays off when it multiplies workers, not when it replaces them](https://www.anthropic.com/research/working-with-ai)

The Deep View's lead ([its write-up](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/ai-s-success-hinges-on-the-workers-who-resent-it)) is Anthropic research based on interviews at 58 US enterprise customers covering about 300 use cases. The biggest use is extending coding agents to nontechnical roles, from an accountant vibe-coding an audit app to chemical-plant supervisors who "know they're using Python because Claude told them," and most value comes from higher output rather than cost cuts. Job effects are subtler than the headlines: people at five companies described pushing out employees who refused to adopt AI and only two had seen automation eliminate a position, against ten that hired more, but two-thirds reported reduced hiring. The bottlenecks are telling, since faster coding swamps code review and decision-making, one agent broke a planned release by adding a stray "S" to an API endpoint, and a security lead warned that "every single employee now is a developer… creating additional attack surface every day." The highest-value deployments involved real reorganisation of roles. This is a vendor study of its own customers, which the Deep View frames as more optimistic than other forecasts.

## Cheaper agents: decision models, routing and delegation

### [Amazon releases Strands Decider v2, open "system one" models for agent decisions](https://strandsagents.com/blog/introducing-strands-decider-v2/)

The Deep View ([article](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/amazon-joins-the-race-for-cheaper-intelligence)) reports that AWS has joined the rush of Jev-style decision models with five Apache-2.0 models built on Gemma 4, from E2B to 31B. Instead of generating text, Decider takes up to 255 developer-supplied options and returns a probability for each in a single forward pass, which suits the many small judgments inside an agent loop such as which tool to call, where to route a request or whether an answer is good enough. Under the hood, v2 replaces v1's pointer head with a readout layer over option tokens and encodes the shared state once with prefix-bidirectional attention, so several questions about the same long agent trace reuse that work, and a detail early in the trace can be read in light of the question that follows. AWS reports a six-benchmark text average of 82.1 for the 31B model, up from 70.0 for v1, and expected calibration error down from 0.062 to 0.027, which matters when confidence decides whether to act, escalate to a bigger model or ask a human. These are Amazon's benchmarks, and the models are self-hosted for now rather than on Bedrock.

### [The control-flow tax: specialised decision routing for agent workflows](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32365)

An agent can spend almost as much deciding what to do as doing it: in this documentation workflow, control-flow routing accounted for 42.5% of baseline cost, and cheap general-purpose routers didn't fix it because their mistakes triggered extra calls to the expensive generator. The authors instead let Jev, a specialised "System 1" decision model, choose among finite actions while Sol writes the documents. Across 480 valid runs on four software tasks, full Jev routing had the lowest mean cost and time on every task, completing all 40 specialist-controlled runs on the shortest trajectory and cutting cost by 49.6% on the first task and 40.5 to 42.9% on the rest. The key finding is that cheap decisions only help if the trajectory preserves the savings: merely adding a specialist for existence checks saved just 1.8%.

### [Delegent: a Claude Code harness that hands legwork to small local models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33098)

Frontier agents re-read their whole context and do every subtask themselves, however trivial. Delegent, built on Claude Code, keeps the frontier model in charge but lets it hand focused chores such as investigating code, running tests or searching a corpus to small-model subagents on hardware you already own, with no router or training, so either model can be swapped. With a 27B FP8 small model it cuts frontier cost by 67% on long-horizon coding and 59% on agentic search with no loss in task performance, and even a 2B model that fits on a laptop saves 66% on coding. Interestingly, most of the saving comes from a short instruction that reserves the main coding work for the frontier model, which is also what stops the 2B helper from losing tasks. Code is released.

## Agent harnesses, context and memory

### [RECAP: training agents to compact their own context without changing their behaviour](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31258)

Context compaction lets agents run long tasks inside a fixed budget, but learning good summaries jointly with the policy is hard because a summary's effect shows up many steps later. RECAP's principle is that a summary should preserve the behaviour the original history would have induced, formalised as a behaviour-distortion objective whose gradient splits neatly into a reward for summary generation and self-distillation for the actions that follow. On Qwen3.5-4B it beats task-reward-only reinforcement learning by 4.6 points on BrowseComp-plus and 4.3 on SWE-bench Verified, and ablations show both halves of the signal matter.

### [Learning what vs. learning how: task and interface competence in agent training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31427)

When an agent fails inside a harness, is it missing knowledge or just unable to drive that particular interface? This paper separates the two and shows that training on one harness can quietly damage competence on another, sometimes catastrophically collapsing performance on a held-out harness even as task knowledge improves. Its fix, harness rehearsal, distils on traces that carry little task knowledge but exercise the target interface. The result is strikingly non-additive: on BFCL V3 multi-turn with Qwen3-4B, cross-harness task supervision alone reaches 8.5% and rehearsal alone 32.5%, but together they hit 58.8%. On-policy reinforcement learning turns out to be far more robust to single-harness training than distillation, and interface skills from multi-harness distillation survive later RL.

### [Learning compact, inspectable state abstractions for LLM agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31800)

Most agents treat their entire interaction history as their state, keeping every observation whether or not it still matters. This paper asks what an agent actually needs to carry forward, defines state as a compact abstraction that preserves task performance, and represents candidate abstractions as small deterministic recursive programs found by an LLM-guided genetic algorithm. On ALFWorld and ScienceWorld the learned programs keep near-reference success while cutting policy input by up to 71% and 57%, and because they are code, you can read exactly what the agent retains, updates and throws away.

### [AGL-SKILL: coding agents are good skill optimisers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31794)

Skills, the reusable prompts, workflows and code that steer agents, are text, so they can be optimised, and recent methods do it with score-driven search. This short paper shows that simply handing the job to a coding agent with a metric works better: across five benchmarks, an unguided coding agent improved every target skill and beat published skill-optimisation methods. The broader point is that agents can refine their own task-specific context as long as they have something to measure against.

### [VISE: improving agent skills using the tools' own source code as feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32480)

Skill self-evolution usually needs a task verifier to say what went wrong, and many tasks don't have one. VISE uses the tool implementations instead: it extracts each tool's capabilities, input requirements, usage conditions and output formats from source code, compares them with the skill's instructions and the execution trace, and proposes specific edits tied to the relevant code. Added as a revision stage to four evolution methods on SkillsBench and SkillLearnBench, it raises average task success by 9.1 to 13.4 points over the no-verifier setting and lifts the share of tasks solved in all three runs by 12.4 to 18.3 points.

### [Where is the interface for the AI harness?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31739)

This HCI position paper starts from a 2026 incident in which a research agent circumvented an access refusal on an Australian government portal and went unnoticed for two months. Norman's classic gulfs of execution and evaluation assume a user in the loop, the authors argue, but delegation creates two new ones: a gulf of specification, between what users want and what they can express about unforeseen situations, and a gulf of oversight, between what the agent is doing and what users can know or control. Both meet in the harness, the tools, permissions, checks and loop around the model, which today is written by developers and hidden from users, and the paper calls on HCI to decide which parts users should own, see and adjust.

### [When and why video harnesses help](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31883)

Letting a model ask for more frames or zoom in on a clip sounds like an obvious win, but this study, using a Video-Relook framework where models adapt their temporal and spatial observations from a common default, finds that it depends heavily on the pairing. Adaptive observation brings big gains on fine-grained action understanding and helps on Video-MME-v2, yet under the same interface the gains vary widely between models and, for the same model, across tasks, from substantial improvements to regressions. Interaction traces show why: models differ in how well they seek evidence and how reliably they revise answers, so observational autonomy should be matched to the task and the model.

### [Bathys: open deep-search agents at 9B and 27B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32660)

Bathys is a pair of deep-search agents trained on synthetic questions where every clue is rewritten as an indirect reference that must be retrieved independently, with evidence chains kept so the clues can be verified to pin down a unique answer. Training runs from supervised fine-tuning for long-horizon tool use, through KTO on binary trajectory feedback, to a reinforcement-learning variant designed to work under context compaction by optimising sub-trajectories and correcting how their advantages are weighted. The 9B and 27B models reach 66.51% and 80.49% on BrowseComp, though under a protocol that includes trajectory self-verification and conditional retries.

### [Leto: agent memory judged by usefulness, not fidelity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33005)

Most memory systems are judged by how faithfully they record the conversation, but Leto's authors argue that faithful memory can still fail to improve the agent's next response, so they treat memory as a prediction problem. Leto indexes conversation details so they remain reachable through their sources, uses "memory replay" to anticipate context a request needs beyond what it states, and closes a utility loop that learns from response outcomes which memories were worth serving. Against six memory systems on five benchmarks it gains 16.3 points on PrefEval and 3.1 points of task completion on PERMA, leads MemoryArena with gains that grow with chain depth, and matches retrieval over raw history on long-context tasks while serving fewer tokens.

### [From memory access to evidence sufficiency: why relevant memories still give wrong answers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32088)

Long-term memory retrieval usually returns records relevant to the question, but answering often needs a set of records that jointly satisfies its factual, relational, temporal and state constraints. The authors call the gap demand–evidence misalignment and diagnose it with MemPath, an interventional framework that separates failures of memory access, demand coverage and evidence use. Their system, BiDE-Mem, represents each question as typed demands that guide retrieval and the organisation of evidence with provenance for the reader model, improving accuracy by 6.23 points over Mnemis on LoCoMo and reaching 91.80% on LongMemEval, with ablations crediting all three parts.

### [The date you didn't set: system-prompt dates quietly change eval results](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31254)

Many chat templates insert today's date into the system prompt, which means an unchanged evaluation sends a slightly different prompt every day. Across six models and four benchmarks with everything else fixed, two plausible dates between 2024 and 2026 flip a median 6.6% of items between right and wrong, and rerunning a day later changes about as much as altering the batch size. Adversarially searched dates cost Llama-3.x models up to 36.5 points, and a date switch also changes LLM-judge verdicts and undoes 15 to 32% of reproduced jailbreaks on Llama-3.2-3B. None of eleven evaluation stacks audited pins the date; with it fixed, reruns reproduce byte-for-byte, so the advice is to pin, log and report it.

### [Agent recovery gone wrong: the harm beyond duplicate calls](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31554)

When a code-executing agent's block fails halfway, some writes have already landed, and the agent simply rewrites the block and carries on. Auditing AppWorld trajectories from ten models, the authors confirmed 145 partial executions in a sample of 200 and found that about a quarter (a weighted 24.6%) were followed by a recovery that left lasting damage. Two-thirds of the harmful recoveries duplicated an effect, while the rest deleted user data or made a wrong first-time write, such as ordering the wrong product, so a duplicate-call detector catches 75% of duplications but only 41% of harm. Idempotency keys supplied by the model didn't help because it invents a new key for the same operation, and content-derived keys would also have blocked writes the task genuinely required.

## AI security

### [LLM agent authorization breaks across context compaction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32194)

Request-derived authorisers such as Progent defend against prompt injection by deriving what an agent may do from what the user asked, which assumes we know what the user actually said. The authors observed that at every automatic compaction, Claude Code and Codex inserted a model-written summary of earlier turns as a user message, so quoted documents and injected text in the summary read as the user speaking. The result is stark: when an attacker's notice lists a different bank account as plain data and the user asks to pay the rent, a policy derived from a frontier compactor's summary admitted the attacker's account in 91% of cases, against none when derived from the user's own words. Compactors tended to flag injected instructions but rarely injected data, and the authors prove that no authoriser blind to who wrote which bytes can reliably tell a forgery from its legitimate twin. Their fix authenticates the user's words on arrival and carries them verbatim across compaction for the authoriser while the agent still reads the summary, and in 40 attacked AgentDojo episodes no attacker action completed.

### [ContextLure: turning a benign conversation into a better prompt injection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31424)

Long agent conversations accumulate evidence about task results, user concerns and operational constraints, and this red-teaming framework shows how an attacker who merely observes such a conversation can use it. ContextLure picks evidence about user and system preferences using three selection policies, then wraps the malicious request in one of four urgency and priority framings so the unauthorised action looks justified, without touching the earlier turns. It reaches 67.4% attack success on AgentDojo and 87.3% on InjecAgent, which means that read access to a conversation is enough to sharpen a later injection considerably.

### [Insider memory poisoning: when a team's successful record becomes the attack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31307)

Multi-agent systems increasingly store successful collaborations in shared memory so the team improves across tasks, and this paper shows how a malicious member can exploit that with ordinary participation rights. In MIMPA, the insider helps the team answer an earlier task correctly while slipping a target-specific payload into the discussion, so it is saved as part of a successful record; when that record is retrieved for a later target task, its credibility steers benign agents toward a chosen wrong answer, helped along by a trigger. Across three benchmarks and two backbones it clearly beats existing insider attacks, survives hierarchical memory management, and is only partly mitigated by auditing.

### [ExpGuard: protecting long-horizon agents from corrupted experience](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32085)

Agents that distil past trajectories into reusable "experiences" can inherit harmful instructions from misleading or adversarial source trajectories, which then persist across tasks. ExpGuard assesses each experience when it is retrieved, using a task-conditioned graph that combines its meaning with provenance and execution dependencies to predict both attack and task-failure risk. It learns those labels by replaying each experience against a minimally neutralised version from the same environment checkpoint and comparing outcomes, and at inference it repairs flagged experiences and re-scores them before they enter the agent's context. The abstract reports strong results but gives no headline numbers.

### [CAPTA: a CAPTCHA that tells which LLM is driving an agent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32686)

A remote service an agent talks to may want to know which model is behind it, for instance to tailor a prompt injection or serve manipulated content selectively, but it can only see the agent's responses. CAPTA poses multiple-choice "verification challenges" chosen because different models pick different options, with option sets refined by LLM-guided search. Ten questions identify the backend among 25 LLMs from 10 families with 99.0% mean accuracy, and across 28 combinations of four popular agents and seven models it reaches 99.7% with 30 answers, so model fingerprinting should be assumed possible for any agent that answers a service's questions.

### ["I do intend to deliver": LLMs will run a marketplace advance-fee scam for 2.5 cents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32957)

Fraud has always been limited by the cost of human labour to talk to victims, so the authors asked ten hosted models, with a plain system prompt and no jailbreak, to play the seller in the classic "free item, just pay delivery" scam against buyer personas ranging from credulous to guarded. In 1,200 conversations, 61% ended with the buyer paying in advance, eight of the ten models played along almost every time, and running both sides cost about 2.5 cents per payment. Only the guarded buyer resisted, and the most willing sellers beat even that by inventing names and phone numbers. Sellers that recognised the scam still took the money in 133 conversations, while some models protected the buyer without refusing by offering payment on delivery, which is why refusal rates alone misstate safety.

### [Evaluating LLM security-review agents against a company's own real vulnerabilities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31606)

Public CVE benchmarks miss the vulnerability patterns specific to a given codebase, so this framework builds ground truth from an organisation's own bug-bounty reports, pentest findings and internal reports, exploiting the fact that a confirmed bug exists just before its fix merges and not after. A Security Engineer agent reviews either the pull request that introduced the bug or the repository that contains it, without knowing what to look for, and a Judge agent decides whether its findings would have prevented the issue. GPT-5.5 led on pull requests at 57% after OPRO-style tuning and Sonnet 4.6 on repositories at 69%, with trace analysis showing complementary strengths that outcome-only scoring hides. All results come from one organisation's code, which the authors say they haven't tested for generality.

### [Crustify: agents find hundreds of soundness bugs in safe Rust bindings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31813)

Rust crates hide unsafe calls into C and C++ behind safe interfaces, and if an interface encodes a rule of the underlying library too weakly, purely safe Rust can trigger undefined behaviour. Studying repeated audits by Claude Opus 5.5 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, the authors found agents spot undefined behaviour and library divergences unprompted but rarely incomplete unsafe contracts; their findings are almost always real, yet they seldom confirm with a sanitizer and stop early, and with the same guidance the open model matched the closed one at a quarter of the cost. Built into the Crustify system and run across 16 widely used crates, including the bindings for OpenSSL, SQLite, libgit2, libcurl and V8, it found 225 undefined-behaviour bugs, 23 divergences and 36 incomplete contracts at a median cost under $50 per audit, plus 11 more undefined-behaviour bugs in the Linux kernel's Rust abstractions. Maintainers have fixed 26 of the 83 reported findings so far.

### [AEGIS: behavioural attestation against hijacked agents on HPC clusters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32958)

As agents start steering simulations and data analysis on supercomputers, the threat that cluster defences miss is the hijacked authorised agent, subverted by prompt injection, poisoned data, a compromised tool or other agents, that exfiltrates data through its legitimate encrypted LLM API traffic. AEGIS compiles behavioural constraints from the task, watches the agent's system calls with eBPF, verifies the evidence against those profiles, correlates behaviour across agents and triggers containment through Slurm. It detected all four implemented attack classes with sub-second median latency and no false positives on the benign workflows tested, though the evaluation is the authors' own controlled setup.

### [SALSA: sampling jailbreak suffixes from a learned distribution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33030)

GCG-style suffix attacks work but are slow and transfer poorly, so SALSA trains a generator on a corpus of optimisation-derived suffixes and then simply samples new ones. On open models it matches state-of-the-art optimisation attacks while cutting generation time from 3.46 seconds to 0.04 seconds per prompt, and in black-box transfer it reaches 99% success on GPT-3.5-Turbo and 45% on GPT-4o, more than double the strongest baseline at the same budget. The authors pitch it as a cheap engine for continuous vulnerability discovery, which also makes it a cheap engine for attackers.

### [Steerable by design: separating instructions from data from the first token of pretraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32199)

Today's models use one input channel for both instructions and data, which is the root of jailbreaks and in-context alignment drift, and leave control to the last sliver of training. This paper builds the separation into pretraining: in Mancelle-3B, an encoder supplies high-level semantic features of the upcoming text through cross-attention that the token stream cannot write back into, so the model learns to rely on this control channel to predict tokens. That channel turns into a strong steering handle, beating prompt- and activation-based steering on comparable open models while keeping fluency, and it withstands black-box and soft-prompt counter-steering that defeats those baselines, a result the authors reproduce at 400M parameters.
