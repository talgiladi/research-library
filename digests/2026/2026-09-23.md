### 1. [Your agent says it works — prove it with custom evals](https://www.decodingai.com/p/evaluate-ai-agents-benchmarks-regression-tests)

Primary link: https://www.decodingai.com/p/evaluate-ai-agents-benchmarks-regression-tests
Paul Iusztin’s Agent Evals 101 lesson for Decode, his educational coding-agent harness, opens with a result that should unsettle anyone shopping models by parameter count: on his custom 19-task benchmark, Qwen3.6-35B hit a 95% pass@1 while GPT-OSS-120B managed only 53% — because the harness had been optimized against the smaller model. That tracks with LangChain’s Terminal-Bench finding that changing only the harness, same model, moved a coding agent from roughly 30th into the top five. Under the hood he treats the eval harness as a separate program that launches the agent as a subprocess (Harbor’s “agent never grades itself” rule), then answers three questions: benchmarks for “does it work,” regression tests for “did we break something else,” and online evals on live traffic. Regression cases grow from production error analysis — observe, diagnose, fix, capture, gate — and score trajectories (which tools fired) rather than just final artifacts, with Opik tracking pass@k, pass^k, and flakiness. The practical punchline is blunt: never trust a generic leaderboard; design a high-signal core set for your own tasks, then let the agent optimize against it.

### 2. [Amazon blocks Meta’s Muse from agentic shopping](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-blocks-metas-muse-ai-assistant-in-new-standoff-over-agentic-shopping/)

Primary link: https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-blocks-metas-muse-ai-assistant-in-new-standoff-over-agentic-shopping/
Barely two weeks after Muse topped the U.S. App Store, Amazon cut the personal agent off from shopping on Amazon.com, showing users a Conditions of Use popup that “continued access by an unauthorized AI agent” violates the rules customers already agreed to. Amazon’s complaint is that Meta never asked, Muse does not identify itself while browsing, and the agent appears to capture and store customer credentials — creating what Amazon casts as an undisclosed third party moving through account pages, order history, and checkout. Meta’s prior line is that Muse has no visibility into passwords or payment methods and that shared credentials sit in secure storage the agent can use without seeing them. The fight is bigger than one app: Amazon has spent a year walling off outside agents (Perplexity’s Comet, Google, OpenAI), and the Ninth Circuit’s Aug. 4 ruling that the user — not the AI company — accesses Amazon’s computers under anti-hacking law pushed Amazon toward contract and terms claims instead. Meanwhile the two companies still partner — Amazon products sell inside Facebook and Instagram, and Meta signed a multibillion-dollar Graviton deal for agentic workloads — so the real contest is who owns the customer relationship when an agent holds the cart.

### 3. [The US–China AI race is more tangled than it looks](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/the-us-china-ai-race-is-more-tangled-than-it-looks)

Primary link: https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/the-us-china-ai-race-is-more-tangled-than-it-looks
Ahead of Xi Jinping’s White House visit, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent floated a U.S.–China “notification mechanism” for AI incidents that could hit national security — framed as moving the two AI powers from opaque to more transparent about shared threats. The Deep View’s point is that the ecosystems are already intertwined, and not always cleanly: Chinese labs such as DeepSeek, Moonshot, and MiniMax face accusations of industrial-scale distillation of U.S. frontier models, while OpenRouter data shows weekly shares of companies routing to Chinese open models swinging between roughly 30% and 46% as a cheaper alternative to frontier APIs, and U.S. data centers still depend on Chinese transformers, batteries, and switchgear even as chips remain contested. Official rhetoric still sells a race — Trump’s AI Action Plan for “global dominance,” Anthropic’s CCP-framed leadership paper, OpenAI’s China-justified buildout — but Info-Tech’s Thomas Randall argues it is better read as a contest for control inside one shared system of suppliers, talent, and research, with each side weaponizing asymmetric exposure. The alert proposal may be the quiet acknowledgment that independence talk and interdependence reality no longer match.

### 5. [Advanced evals: find (and fix) hidden AI product failures](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/advanced-evals-how-to-find-and-fix)

Primary link: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/advanced-evals-how-to-find-and-fix
Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar’s Lenny sequel argues most AI teams skip the stage that matters most: error discovery. Drawing on work with 50+ companies, they say jumping straight to metrics measures the wrong things; Shopify, Cursor, Ramp, and Harvey all used evals to drive real product gains (faster/cheaper builders, higher satisfaction, precision jumps, quality doubles), but only after figuring out which failures are worth tracking. Their three-step loop with a coding agent — instrument traces, review and annotate in a custom local app the agent builds for your schema, then cluster failure modes into product priorities — takes about 30 minutes once you know it, with a deliberate first-10-trace human pass before accepting agent suggestions so product judgment (e.g. a leasing bot that politely ends a budget objection instead of offering cheaper units) is not automated away. The companion evals-skills plugin (`npx skills add https://github.com/ai-evals-course/evals-skills`) routes into `/evals-start` and error-discovery workflows so Claude or Codex handle sampling, clustering, and interface tweaks while you stay the judge of what “good” means as criteria drift.

### 7. [How OpenAI built GPT-Live full-duplex voice](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/how-openai-built-gpt-live)

Primary link: https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/how-openai-built-gpt-live
ByteByteGo sits down with OpenAI voice engineers Zahan Malkani and WebRTC creator Justin Uberti to unpack GPT-Live-1, the July 2026 full-duplex family that listens and speaks at once instead of waiting on a turn detector. The arc runs cascaded ASR→LLM→TTS (information loss and stacked latency), turn-based speech-to-speech (tone preserved, but pause detection still cuts people off), then full-duplex: continuous audio tokens on a fixed clock, with silence as just another token — Moshi-style, roughly one frame every 80 ms. OpenAI’s production trick is separating talking from thinking: a small fast voice model stays on the conversation while a frontier model (e.g. GPT-5.5) handles search and hard reasoning on an async path, so the user never sits in dead air. Serving splits live audio from everything else; WARP collapses WebRTC’s six setup round-trips into one; conversations stay hot in GPU memory with managed handoffs when instances recycle; and evals shift from turn scores to endpointing/barge-in behavior, stream health at p999 rather than p95, and silent launches against production traffic. The next bet they sketch is voice driving a computer — screenshots, long tasks, mid-flight redirects — while keeping the live conversation smooth.

## Agent memory

### [Compressing Long Context into Answer-Aligned Memory Embeddings for LLM Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25537)

Rahman, Amin, Mia, Watanobe, and Liu attack long-context cost—quadratic attention plus a growing KV cache—without retraining the decoder. Their CMC method turns long inputs into compact Context Memory Embeddings in a frozen decoder’s embedding space, served through a two-tier KV cache that mixes question-guided selection with a local context window. The compressor trains via answer-targeted distillation, so memory optimizes for what the model will say, not generic reconstruction. Across nine encoder–decoder pairings and four QA benches, CMC gains up to 7.3 EM / 4.0 F1 on SQuAD while cutting time and energy by as much as 20% and peak GPU memory by up to 50% at 3,000 generation tokens. Soft-compression note for Tal: answer-align the embeddings, freeze the decoder, and let the query pick compressed slots.

### [Qwen3.8-Omni: Towards Native Omni-Modal Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25611)

The Qwen Team’s Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash pitches a native omni-modal agent for long-horizon work across text, audio, and video rather than another perception demo. It inherits Qwen3.8-Next’s sparse MoE stack, stretches the context window to one million tokens, and uses native multimodal co-training so agentic tool-use habits learned in text transfer into audio and video instead of collapsing at the modality boundary. Beside the token count, the interesting claim is memory management for marathon sessions where transcript, screen, and mic keep growing together. Relative to earlier omni releases scored on “can it see/hear,” this one is judged on whether it finishes multimodal agent jobs. Omni-agent note for Tal: meter the 1M window as a memory budget, and test whether co-trained tool use survives mid-task modality switches.

### [LADDER: Graph-Guided Diffusion Language Models for Efficient Multi-Hop Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24346)

Zhang, Luo, Zhang, An, Dong, Zhang, and Sun want GraphRAG’s multi-hop reasoning without autoregressive decoding’s sequential choke. LADDER pairs diffusion LMs’ refine-in-parallel drafts with graph-guided retrieval after fixing two problems: half-denoised text is a moving grounding target, and noisy intermediates make synchronous multi-hop retrieval ruinously expensive. An event-driven self-clock rests on a sharp observation—about 88% of target entities already appear early in the draft, leading final commitment by roughly 5.7–9.6 steps—so retrieval fires only when the set of graph-linkable entities expands. The asynchronous policy skips learned gates and brittle heuristics while keeping GraphRAG’s topology in the loop. RAG speed note for Tal: clock retrieval off entity emergence in the draft, not a fixed diffusion schedule.

### [Data Agents: Agentic Data Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24137)

Li, Zhou, Zhou, Sun, Luo, and Fan argue classical data stacks are the wrong shape for the AI era: human-written pipelines, literal handling of messy sources, and purely reactive execution. Their Data Agent paradigm treats the database as an autonomous worker that organizes data semantically, runs semantic operators, orchestrates and optimizes pipelines, refines itself from feedback, manages memory, and adapts proactively instead of waiting on the next cron. Memory management sits beside orchestration as a first-class component—not a RAG afterthought—so the agent retains schema knowledge, failures, and prior plans across jobs. Two specialized agents on the stack show the pattern in practice. Data-plane note for Tal: if the warehouse drives itself, give it an explicit memory layer for schema semantics and pipeline scars.

### [Taramandal-GPT: Enhancing Astrodynamics Problem-Solving with Knowledge Retrieval and Structured Thinking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24246)

Sharma, Gupta, and Abidi answer a familiar complaint—general LLMs stumble on multi-step symbolic astrodynamics—with Taramandal-GPT (“Constellation-GPT”): a Qwen3-8B backbone wrapped in a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline plus a fallback when context is thin. They evaluate on APBench’s 299 space-science questions using both numeric margin scoring and semantic similarity, so a nearly-right orbital answer is not graded like a confident hallucination. Against strong open and closed models the domain-adapted RAG stack stays competitive, which matters for specialists who cannot wait for a frontier model to memorize every perturbation equation. Domain-RAG note for Tal: pair a mid-size open backbone with curated retrieval and a dual numeric/semantic grader when a wrong constant means a wrong trajectory.

### [Beyond Predictable Paths: Redefining AI Security Incident Reporting for Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24515)

Pustozerova, Bagdasarian, Beurer-Kellner, Biggio, and a long expert roster ask what an incident report must contain when the casualty is an AI agent rather than a classical AI system. Drawing on 23 experts, they separate agents from systems and list the fields that matter once autonomy, tools, and memory enter the blast radius—among them agent memory and memory accesses, actual versus potential autonomy, and tool usage. The contribution is the checklist: compliance forms still read as if the model only answered a chat box. Open questions follow on recording incidents efficiently and when vulnerabilities generalize across stacks. Incident-schema note for Tal: demand memory-access logs and tool-use lineage beside the prompt transcript.

## Agent harness

### [How Strongly Should Task State Influence an LLM Agent?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25686)

Zhang, Kweon, and Han isolate a harness question most demos conflate: how much long-horizon reliability comes from showing task state, telling the model what to do next, or enforcing a state machine? Holding rules, model, and episodes fixed, they vary influence from a raw transcript, to an exact checklist, to per-turn directives from a receipt-advanced state machine, to a hard gate that refuses state-violating actions—scoring by exact payload match. Across three models and two domains, displaying accurate state is unreliable; an unverified ledger the agent writes itself beats a checklist it is shown; directives help only with obedience; enforcement needs no obedience but is bounded by state correctness. State-channel note for Tal: compile a receipt-driven machine and gate illegal actions at the harness—don’t trust “it’s in the prompt.”

### [Self-Healing Harness for Runtime Oversight of Agent Self-Modification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24130)

Tayebati, Kumar, Darabi, Krishnan, and Trivedi treat agent self-modification as admission control: the model may propose new operating instructions, but an external runtime gate decides what persists. Their model-agnostic self-healing harness wraps an unmodified agent in Detect → Notice → Heal → Validate. Candidate rules land in an external workspace with only provisional authority; they earn cross-episode persistence only after measured improvement on the triggering failure without regressing beyond a fixed margin on protected cases. Replay supplies matched evidence when available; forward trials are the weaker fallback. The punchline is governance without forking weights. Self-mod note for Tal: let the agent draft habit patches, but keep persistence behind a harness that demands before/after proof on the motivating failure.

### [EDGEGEN: Improving Tool-Calling Agents Beyond Happy Paths with Synthetic Edge Case Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24115)

Abichandani, Chong, Shen, Singh, Hathidara, and collaborators notice synthetic tool-calling datasets ignore the agent’s real state and database, so “diverse” tasks still look like happy paths. EdgeGen extracts compliance rules from the agent’s specification and deliberately generates database-grounded tasks meant to violate those rules, then folds them into finetuning and harness optimization in a closed loop with no human labels. On τ²-bench’s airline domain, EdgeGen finetuning yields mean progress gains of about 2–42% where other synthetics sometimes degrade; harness optimization on Gemma-4-e4b beats a human-curated harness by ~10% and the base by ~30%. Edge-case note for Tal: synthesize the tasks that break your own policy text, then spend the wins on both weights and harness edits.

### [Trains but Doesn't Learn: A Post-Training Delivery Benchmark for LLM Agents as Forward-Deployed Engineers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25237)

Ding and Zhan reframe post-training-as-a-service as trust: the customer hands over data and a goal; an LLM agent in the forward-deployed-engineer seat must return a fine-tuned, evaluated, deployed model under budget, approval, and reproducibility constraints. Their governed delivery plane scores ten stages from platform-recorded facts. The signature silent failure is TBDL—trains but doesn’t learn—where loss falls, dashboards stay green, and the delivered model matches the base. An operator acceptance gate catches every such run before payment; a detector calibrated on corrupted runs flags severe cases mid-flight. Four frontier agents were compared end-to-end against a human FDE under the same oracle across 8B–70B bases. PTaaS note for Tal: score the delivery plane, not the training curve, and refuse payment on green-loss / flat-eval runs.

### [Jev-Mem: System-One-Controlled Agentic Memory for Efficient AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23986)

Jiang, Li, and Li import System-One / System-Two cognition into agentic memory so a slow autoregressive LLM is no longer on the critical path of every remember/retrieve decision. Jev-Mem splits into a System-One control plane, a structured multi-relational memory plane, and a System-Two reasoning plane: the fast controller handles memory typing, relational organization, query routing, retrieval-budget allocation, graph traversal, candidate scoring, and adaptive stopping, while the deliberative model only reasons once the shortlist is ready. It is a natural cousin of yesterday’s Jev decision-model story—cheap typed control for memory ops, expensive generation reserved for thinking. Memory-control note for Tal: move organize/retrieve/stop off the generative loop before you scale the transcript store.

### [DUMA-Bench: A Dual-Control Multi-Agent Benchmark for Evaluating LLM Agent Security](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24662)

Aleksandrov, Kochnev, Sadiekh, and Rogoza argue most agent security evals assume a passive user and a static control loop. DUMA-Bench extends τ²-bench into dual-control interaction—both agent and user can rewrite shared state—and plants adversarial environments across eight vulnerability classes including RAG poisoning, cross-agent manipulation, and unsafe output handling. Fourteen models from OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Z.ai run across eight domains and multiple user-behavior regimes; turning on dual-control reliably worsens security outcomes versus the passive-user baseline. The harness lesson is uncomfortable: your single-agent red team is optimistic. Dual-control note for Tal: evaluate under an adversarial co-controller that touches the same state, not only under canned prompt injections.

## LLM security

### [Universal Fractal Natural Language Decision Map: Real-Time Edge Triage Across Heterogeneous Domains](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25498)

Dağlı, Dağlı, and Dağlı push a Mandelbrot-fractal “decision map”—cousin to System-One / Jev typed noul/choice/score outputs—onto a zero-VRAM edge runtime (werr / answerr.me) that synthesizes decisions from 24-byte coordinate seeds rather than weight tensors. Beside the latency pitch, they claim a spectral-density property that empirically blocked prompt-injection bypasses in their tests (0.0% bypass, wide Wilson CI) while preserving triage across domains via an Auto-Seed Router. Treat the security number cautiously given the interval, but the framing matters: deterministic System-One classifiers are being sold partly as injection-resistant reflexes in front of heavier LLMs. Edge-triage note for Tal: if a Jev-like layer sits in front of tools, measure injection bypass with honest intervals and keep the LLM off the authorization path.
