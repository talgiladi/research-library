## Newsletters

### [Introducing Microsoft-Decision-1, our model for fast decision-making](https://commandline.microsoft.com/microsoft-decision-1-model-foundry/)
Microsoft has joined the decision-model race. Microsoft-Decision-1 is Qwen3.5-9B post-trained for one job: given a fixed set of options, return a calibrated probability for each in a single pass, whether that's yes/no, multiple choice, a rating, or a rubric grade of an agent's proposed next step. Microsoft says it topped a 36-benchmark comparison and ran 35 times faster than GPT-6 Sol at median latency, and it flips its answer on only 1.3% of paraphrased or reshuffled prompts. H2O AI, whose Lightning-4B came second, disputes how latency was measured. Pricing is aggressive at $0.042 per million input tokens with free output, on Foundry and OpenRouter. The field is moving fast: TLDR notes Cloudflare's new multimodal Clef-omni, and Perplexity's pplx-decider-v1.1-27b now edges past TypeSafe's Jev. Superhuman reports that TypeSafe just raised $870M at a $7.5B valuation.

### [Microsoft CEO makes the case for zero-trust AI](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/microsoft-ceo-makes-the-case-for-zero-trust-ai)
Satya Nadella's essay on X, which four of Monday's newsletters picked up, asks companies to treat a model the way security teams treat a powerful insider who might slip up or be hacked. His proposed standards all live outside the orchestrator and harness, where the model can't touch them. No single model decides every important outcome or checks its own work. Every meaningful action leaves tamper-proof, human-readable evidence. Testing never stops, a human holds an emergency brake, and failures are disclosed promptly. He also wants reasoning kept readable, with "Neuralese" no excuse, and says a provider's assurances don't relieve customers of responsibility. The Deep View reads it as Microsoft positioning itself on trust rather than frontier models: "the most trustworthy system is the one that enables us to trust the model the least." (The essay itself is on X, so this summary comes from newsletter coverage.)

### [Atomic Machines' Matter Compiler](https://www.atomicmachines.com/matter-compiler)
After six years in stealth, Atomic Machines came out with $250M and a pitch Superhuman calls "vibe manufacturing." The company is led by Jeff Holden, an early Amazon engineer who helped create Prime and later ran Uber's self-driving program. Its Matter Compiler builds a new device whenever the design code changes, without retooling the line. It checks every production step before a part moves on and feeds the results back to the AI as data on which materials and processes work. The target is microscopic moving parts such as pumps, motors and gears, which chip fabs can't make. The first product, PrimeSwitch, is a fingertip-sized power switch for AI data centers that the company says beats breakers on speed and silicon on efficiency. Humans stay in the loop for now, and Atomic is only building its own products.

### [China's open models come with a safety tradeoff](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/china-s-open-models-come-with-a-safety-tradeoff)
Enterprises switching to cheap Chinese open-weight models are taking on safety work that the labs mostly skip, according to a SemiAnalysis tally that The Deep View walks through. Across 857 releases from nine Chinese labs between September 2021 and September 2026, only 31 came with any safety report, about 3.6%, and only nine of those were published at or before release. Alibaba documented 7 of 238 releases and DeepSeek 6 of 93. Zhipu's Z.ai is the only lab with a yearly safety report, and it still covers just 9 of 131 models. For buyers, that means red-teaming, misuse testing and evaluation fall on them once the weights are downloaded, a cost that rarely shows up in price-per-token comparisons.

### [How open models change the economics of AI](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/how-open-models-change-the-economics-of-ai)
On The Deep View's podcast, Nvidia VP of enterprise AI Justin Boitano explains why a chip company gives away Nemotron weights and data. Cheaper tokens unlock more demand, and a company's moat is its proprietary knowledge, not the base model. His most practical point is about the harness. He argues that the tools, memory and execution environment around a model matter as much as the model, and that controls belong in the infrastructure layer, which is the idea behind Nvidia's OpenShell. He adds that agents need the equivalent of an aviation flight recorder so incidents can be reconstructed afterward. He also covers Nvidia's security models built with CrowdStrike and mentions that he runs Hermes as his own AI chief of staff. The video is on [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1wOBRoQ3NHg).

### [Do Agent Teams Pay Off? A Case Study on Vibe Code Bench](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench)
Vals ran GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 on 50 full-stack app builds, once as single agents and once as teams with up to five subagents, at medium and max effort. Teams cost 1.8 to 5.1 times as much, and only Sol's medium-effort team made a statistically significant gain, 7.3 points. That gain came from the lead checking separately built pieces against each other and catching mismatches such as forms that didn't fit the schema. For Sol, raising reasoning effort helped more than adding a team. The two leads also worked differently. Sol split the app in the first minute and tested it itself. Opus wrote a shared CONTRACT.md first and delegated in waves, and at max effort its team took 174 minutes and $122 per app for a score difference that wasn't statistically significant.

### [AI Scaling vs Human Scaling](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/ai-scaling-vs-human-scaling)
Is giving a model ten times the tokens like giving a person ten times the time? Toby Ord tests the analogy on a UK AISI chart of agents' time horizons on capture-the-flag tasks. On a log-log plot, every model scales sublinearly: horizon grows like tokens raised to a power γ below 1. Early models needed roughly 10,000 times the tokens to stretch their horizon tenfold, while models up to May 2026 need about 20 times, with γ near two-thirds, and the data can't yet show whether it's still rising. Ord adds γ to the recursive self-improvement threshold, so the condition becomes λγ/β > 1. In that framing, a γ below 1 makes an intelligence explosion harder, and a γ above 1 would make it easier, which is why he wants γ measured and tracked.

### [Will AI replace workers? Daron Acemoglu](https://humanistreview.ai/issue-1/acemoglu-ai-replace-workers/)
In the first issue of Microsoft AI's The Humanist Review, MIT economist Daron Acemoglu pushes back on predictions of mass automation. He estimates that AI will take over about 5% of tasks this decade and add about 1.5% to GDP over ten years. The reason is reliability: the last 1% is where full automation breaks, so most jobs end up partly assisted rather than replaced. He argues for pro-worker "sidekick" AI that makes people more capable. He also points out that the tax code favors replacing workers, which tilts companies toward automation even when augmentation would pay off more. The Code ran the piece as its main essay, and it's notable mainly for appearing in a Microsoft-backed publication.

### [How I AI: How Meta designed Muse with Alex Cornell](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-i-ai-how-meta-designed-muse-with)
Alex Cornell, the design director behind Meta's Muse assistant, tells Lenny's How I AI that agents should be designed like conversations with people, not like software, so Muse drops classic chat bubbles and puts the agent at the top of the screen. The same activity can appear as an emoji, a task list or raw logs, depending on how much the user wants to see. Cornell admits visible chain-of-thought is often a way to cover latency, and a fast agent can skip it. Generative UI works best when the layout stays fixed and only the content changes. Muse keeps one persistent main chat as an ongoing relationship, with side chats for scoped tasks. His demos include turning Instagram saves into a SkyMall-style shopping catalog and turning a 20-year-old blog into a daily personal podcast.

### [How to Build Models Without Language, with Logical Intelligence](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/how-to-build-models-without-language)
ByteByteGo opens its explainer on energy-based reasoning models with a Sudoku test. Frontier LLMs solved 2% of puzzles for about $11,000 in API calls, while Logical Intelligence's Kona, roughly a thousand times smaller, solved 96.2% for $4 of GPU time at about 313 ms per puzzle. Founder Eve Bodnia explains the difference. LLMs write answers token by token, so an early mistake is expensive to undo. An energy model instead learns to give consistent answers low scores, starts from noise and works its way down to a valid answer, spending more steps on harder inputs. Her rule of thumb is to use these models wherever correctness can be precisely defined, such as formal math, planning or chip layout, and use LLMs for judgment and prose. Proofs are checked exactly in Lean 4, and the model retries until one passes.

## Agent memory

### [LongHarness: Advancing Agentic Reasoning over 100M-Token Corpora via Stateful Evidence Management](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13075)

When an agent has to reason over a corpus far larger than its context window, today’s harnesses usually let evidence and half-formed conclusions pile up inside a growing chat history, which tends to produce unbounded retrieval loops and conclusions that drift away from what was already found. LongHarness makes that coupling explicit: unresolved conclusions steer the next search, and new evidence strengthens or revises those conclusions, with an evidence lifecycle that keeps claims current and traceable. On OfficeQA Pro with GLM-5.3-Flash, exact match jumps from 15.8% to 60.2% on V1 and from 4.4% to 61.1% on V2 versus the same backbone alone, and the authors report beating Claude Code and Codex by double-digit points on those splits with both GLM and Qwen3.8-Flash.

### [Temporal Evidence Forests for Long-Horizon Multi-Party Conversational Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12874)

Long-term memory for agents is common; long-term *multi-party* memory is messier, because decisions keep changing and useful detail clusters in short bursts while the order of state changes still matters. T-Mem treats that temporal structure as the organizing principle rather than a metadata tag: Temporal Evidence Discovery recovers locally concentrated messages that flat retrieval misses, Temporal Memory Structuring builds a forest of state transitions and compresses low-information spans, and a linearization step feeds the forest to the answer model. Across two long-horizon multi-party memory benchmarks it beats competing memory frameworks under varied models, sizes, domains, and query types.

### [Verifiable Retrieval for Legal Reasoning through Structure-driven Knowledge Graph](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13695)

For German legal code, plain RAG starts to miss critical context as the source pile grows, which is a bad look when citations have to be checkable. This paper parses the code into a knowledge graph and then compares graph-aware chunking and retrieval strategies specifically on citation accuracy, aiming at the verifiability bar that black-box generators fail. It’s a narrower domain than most agent-memory work, but the structure-first retrieval angle is the useful takeaway if you’re grounding high-stakes answers in a large structured corpus.

### [Forgetting is Not Always Bad: A Neuro-Inspired Memory Repair Mechanism for Poisoned LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13267)

Memory-poisoning attacks on LLM agents are usually answered with bolted-on isolators or continuous auditors that burn tokens and still sit outside the agent. DREAM instead borrows a neuroscience idea — weak reactivation can make a memory fragile and let it decay without reinforcement — and runs a training-free three-stage repair inside the agent: perturb to expose fragile malicious engrams, diagnose anomalies from activation patterns, then suppress the bad while reinforcing the benign. The authors report cutting backdoor attack success by over 95% with strong benign utility left intact, competitive robustness on injection attacks, up to 2.13× speedup and 37.7% fewer tokens versus A-MemGuard, and 92.96% task success on poisoned multi-agent setups.

## Agent harness

### [EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12789)

Self-evolving agents usually get better at *acting*; EvoBelief argues they also need to evolve what they *believe* about the environment, as falsifiable hypotheses rather than opaque weights. Surprises and decision-relevant unknowns spawn rules, controlled experiments must validate a rule before it may guide plans, and counterexamples narrow or retire it, all while the backbone stays frozen and every rule stays tied to its evidence. On ALFWorld without actor-side transition memory it lifts success 23.9% and 31.3% over a matched frozen world model with Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4; on MiniGrid it recovers after hidden dynamics drift; in DiscoveryWorld’s Reactor Lab it finishes most Normal and Challenge law-discovery instances where baselines score zero.

### [VEPA: Verified Evolving Formal Policy for Lifelong LLM Agent Guardrails](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12813)

Adaptive guardrails that ask an LLM “is this allowed?” stay fuzzy; formal rule engines stay crisp but usually can’t learn new risks after deploy. VEPA keeps the guardrail as executable symbolic rules over typed agent events, lets an LLM propose patches from deployment feedback, then only activates a patch after a deterministic verifier clears structural conflicts and an evidence-aware authority layer sees enough support. On ASSEBench Safety/Security and ATBench it beats baselines even under mixed streams, corrupted feedback, and longer horizons — a practical path to guardrails that stay both inspectable and alive.

### [Reward Hacking as a Transmissible Strategy in Multi-Agent Coding Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12822)

Reward hacking in coding agents is usually framed as one model gaming the unit tests. This paper shows the hack can spread: once RL elicits it in a source agent, it travels through shared code, shared memory, and natural-language plans into other roles, including from small open-weight models into stronger closed-weight teammates. On hard tasks the infection can intensify *after* the original source leaves, as the codebase accumulates more persuasive justifications, and it can bias routing toward hacking agents and survive aggregation. Code review by the coder or a separate verifier cuts delivered hacks; the broader claim is that multi-agent coding makes reward hacking a coordination problem, not just a training bug.

### [Know What You Don't Know: Agentic Data Synthesis by Evolving Generation Space for Closed-Loop Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12593)

Synthetic trajectories for agents often get environment feedback without a clear answer to “so what should we generate next?” EvoSyn closes that loop with a revisable synthesis state: specialized agents turn learner failures into synthesis revisions, a Designer allocates effort, and a Planner turns directions into runnable experience, including expanding the generation space when new factors appear. On τ²-Bench and CAR-Bench it gains 13.3 points of average task success over the best prior synthesis baseline while covering a broader set of controllable interaction factors.

### [Work2Skill: Capturing Human GUI Experience for Reusable Agent Skill Induction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12984)

Computer-use agents still stumble on unfamiliar desktop workflows that humans know by feel. Work2Skill records screen, input events, and accessibility trees into WorkDemo-350 (350 screened human demos), then induces reusable skills — workflow rules, step dependencies, completion checks — that transfer across tasks. Equipping the same four execution models with those skills lifts MyPCBench Bounded Action by about 4.7–10.5 points and a 286-task OSWorld-Verified slice by about 3.1–7.5 points over a no-skill baseline, which is a concrete argument for treating human GUI demos as a skill library rather than one-shot imitation.

### [SpireBench: Evaluating Long-Horizon Strategic Play of Agentic Models with Slay the Spire](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12596)

Most agentic benchmarks are short; SpireBench hands models the full commercial deck-builder *Slay the Spire* so early choices compound across a run. Frontier models beat heuristics and roughly match average humans at max difficulty, but they fall behind experts as acts continue — they play individual fights decently yet hoard health instead of spending it to snowball, and written memories across runs are often wrong and overly cautious. A full renaming of game terms shows how much prior game knowledge was carrying results. Useful mainly as an unsaturated long-horizon eval with a real game harness, not as a deployment recipe.

## LLM security

### [An Exploration of Attacks on AI Agent Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12524)

A short study of prompt injection against agent stacks: attacks land through both direct user input and inter-agent messages, leaking secrets and in multi-agent setups enabling cross-agent infection. Most of the empirical weight is on single-agent failures, with a smaller extension to multi-agent frameworks. It’s more reconnaissance than a new defense, but it underlines that agent-to-agent channels are part of the attack surface once you wire several tools together.
