## Newsletters

### [Introducing Muse Spark Open](https://research.meta.ai/blog/muse-spark-open)
Meta has released the weights of Muse Spark Open, which it calls the most capable US open-weight model to date: a natively multimodal mixture with 816B total and 42B active parameters and a million-token context window, aimed at coding and long-running agent work. The headline trick is local deployment. Working with Apple, Meta tuned it for MLX so that two M5 Ultra Mac Studios, each offering up to 512 GB of unified memory, act as one terabyte machine, and it demonstrated on-device runs that shipped iOS fixes and edited video in DaVinci Resolve. Hosted APIs launched with seven providers, and weights sit on Hugging Face and the major clouds under a license with limited commercial terms. Kimi K3 still leads on coding benchmarks.

### [Frontier Pre-training at Microsoft AI](https://microsoft.ai/news/frontier-pre-training-at-microsoft-ai/)
Microsoft AI has finished pre-training MAI-Base-2, a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 80 billion active parameters, and says it beats every open-weight model it tested on held-out evaluations. The more striking claim is efficiency: its recipe is estimated to be 4 to 12 times more compute-efficient than DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base, a saving it reinvested in a bigger model. Training ran on 16,000 GB200 GPUs for 38 days. The write-up credits scaling ladders, a dropless MoE design with fixed memory use, the Muon optimizer, and clipping gradients at activations rather than weights. Microsoft stresses that the data is organic rather than distilled from other models, which it says will let it license the weights to enterprises through Foundry. Post-training is still under way.

### [An update on independent evaluators](https://www.anthropic.com/news/an-update-on-independent-evaluators)
Anthropic has given a six-person team from the nonprofit METR what it calls employee-like access: company hardware, office space and internal tools roughly on a par with its own risk-assessment staff. METR can publish its analysis without Anthropic's approval, though Anthropic may require redactions in a few defined categories, such as customer data, privileged legal material and model architecture, and it decides what falls into them. The post sets out a three-tier scheme for evaluators, with METR the first at the top tier, and mentions SecureBio, Accenture and Faculty as other partners. It compares the setup to resident bank examiners. The Deep View noted the arrangement is voluntary and set against a Reuters report that labs seek control over what evaluators publish.

### [The Beauty of Something Grown](https://www.hyperdimensional.co/p/the-beauty-of-something-grown)
Dean Ball uses Goodfire researcher Nick Cammarata's new essay, Neural Networks Are Already Clean, to ask what happens if interpretability turns out to be tractable soon. Cammarata now argues that polysemantic neurons, long blamed on superposition, mostly reflect training that layers meanings over time, which he calls recursive generalization; neurons are better read as control flow than as human nouns, and the famous 2017 cat-and-car neuron really detects V shapes. If so, agents could scale the analysis and alignment would look more like engineering. Ball cautions that it leans on Cammarata's judgment pending a paper, and that single neurons are not whole-model interactions. His most interesting move is political: a breakthrough would split safety advocates and, he says, push him toward full steam ahead.

Cognition has added Oncall to Devin, an agent that takes production pages and bug reports, digs through logs and code, and then either escalates with a severity rating and a likely root cause or opens a tested pull request. ### [Context Inspector: Measuring Context Quality Before the LLM Call](https://www.decodingai.com/p/pre-inference-rag-evaluation-metrics)
American Express engineers Thangavel Subramaniam and Saurabh Gupta argue in a Decoding AI guest post that many RAG failures happen before the model writes a token, when context is assembled. Their research prototype, Context Inspector, scores the retrieved context with six numbers: coverage of required answer aspects, token utilization against a healthy band, missing required sources, contradiction density between chunks, freshness with exponential decay, and policy admissibility. Those signals feed a pass or fail gate and a subset-selection step, and the post includes Python for each metric plus a CI pattern that fails the build when retrieval, chunking or ranking changes push a threshold. It complements post-inference evaluation rather than replacing it, and can run on metadata alone for privacy. Examples are synthetic.

### [EP229: 8 Chunking strategies for RAG-based agents](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/ep229-8-chunking-strategies-for-rag)
ByteByteGo's weekly roundup leads with eight ways to chunk documents for retrieval, running from fixed-size windows with overlap through recursive and heading-based splitting to semantic chunking that cuts where sentence-embedding similarity drops. At the more expensive end sit LLM-generated propositions, agentic chunking where an agent picks a method per file type, late chunking that embeds the whole document before pooling per boundary, and hierarchical sections-to-sentences indexes. The issue also sketches how Google Translate works, from byte-pair tokens and a target-language token through an encoder-decoder Transformer and beam search, explains Agent Skills as a small index the model consults before loading a SKILL.md, and contrasts PostgreSQL's process-per-connection design with MySQL's threads. Note: one listed item, on testing AI-written code, was image-only in the email.

### [What on earth is going on with AI pricing and packaging?](https://open.substack.com/pub/elenaverna/p/what-on-earth-is-going-on-with-ai)
Lovable's Elena Verna, in a post cross-published by Lenny's Newsletter, tours the pricing pages of the major AI products and finds them bewildering. Plans are expressed as multiples of an undisclosed base, credits nobody can translate, or vague tiers like limited, more and maximum, and she traces this to four problems: credit and token currencies that hide real cost, the mistaken idea that bigger numbers signal value, no obvious unit of consumption for general-purpose agents, and unpredictable inference costs. Outcome-based pricing is attractive but hard to deliver. Her conclusion is that early adopters will tolerate the confusion while the mass market will not, so whoever makes AI pricing understandable and predictable will win. Note: the post's examples are drawn from the newsletter excerpt.

## Agent memory

### [MemCut: Every Secret Leaves a Trail in the Long-Term Memory of LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21405)

Most agent-memory research chases better retrieval, but a memory that cannot forget on request is a privacy liability. MemCut is a benchmark of 456 hand-designed secrets planted in long conversations, each with an annotated evidence trail, so that recovery can be tested after a user asks for deletion, including from messages that never state the secret outright. The authors compare what a reader recovers through the system's own retrieval with what it can still reconstruct from surviving traces, across five memory designs and four models. Removing the disclosure itself cuts native recovery far more than residual recovery, which suggests that deleting one memory entry rarely makes an agent forget.

### [MemComposer: Learning to Assemble Memories for LLM Agents via Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21091)

Semantic similarity decides which memories an agent retrieves, yet it says little about which ones actually help. MemComposer adds a learned step between retrieval and prompt: given the current query and the candidates, it decides which experiences to include, how many, and in what order. It is trained with a GRPO-style method called Experience Policy Optimization that scores each assembled memory context by running complete agent interactions and rewarding task success. Across three tau-squared-bench domains with two frozen executors it beats the strongest baseline for each while using 16.9% and 4.9% fewer memory tokens, and the learned policy transfers to unseen domains, executors and memory banks.

### [Beyond Downstream QA: Rethinking How We Evaluate Agent Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22209)

Agent-memory benchmarks usually ask questions and score the answers, a shortcut that blurs the quality of the memory with the quality of retrieval and generation. The authors argue this produces false positives, where a good answer hides a broken store, and false negatives, where a retrieval failure blames sound memory. Their FEB-30 benchmark builds 30 trajectories of evolving facts by generating documents, emails and conversations from known ground truth, which makes it possible to inspect the memory store after every ingestion step. On a representative system, store-level and answer-level evaluation diverged by an average of 16 percentage points, a gap worth remembering the next time a memory leaderboard moves.

### [Hy-Memory-30B-A3B: Attributed Reinforcement Learning for Long-Term Memory Writing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22196)

The write path decides what a memory system keeps, and it is normally handled by a general-purpose LLM hidden behind framework-specific prompts, which is costly and hard to port. Hy-Memory-30B-A3B is a compact writer trained to work across Mem0, MemOS and Hy-Memory. Memory on-policy distillation first teaches each framework's write protocol, and reinforcement learning then optimizes downstream answerability with per-chunk probes, cross-chunk credit attribution and controls against over-storing. Training carries the model's own written banks across windows so it matches deployment. The authors report that it ranks first on fixed subsamples of the benchmarks they test.

### [All-in-One Memory: Process-Rewarded Reinforcement Learning for Memory Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21147)

Memory methods typically tune retrieval and memory construction separately, leaving search, writing and stopping decisions without a common yardstick. AiO-Mem treats them as transitions over one shared evidence state built from source-traceable, multi-view events compiled out of long histories such as multi-session dialogues. A single policy rolls out whole interaction trajectories, while a separate process reward model, trained with supervised fine-tuning and trajectory-level DPO, scores each action; those scores are combined into an action-balanced reward for optimizing the policy. The pitch is that reading, writing and knowing when to stop can be learned together instead of being stitched from separate heuristics.

### [Librarian Agents: Training LLMs to Curate Long-Context Workspaces that Anticipate Future Queries](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22273)

Retrieval is reactive: it waits for a question and then goes digging. This paper starts from an asymmetry in agent harnesses such as Claude Code, where models are good at navigating an existing filesystem but are rarely trained to build one for later use. A builder agent turns raw documents into a hierarchical filesystem of synthetic files and reference links, a workspace tailored for a downstream answering agent, and the construction is framed as a Markov decision process optimized by reinforcement learning. It is a notable twist on agent memory: instead of storing facts, the agent learns to organize a corpus ahead of the questions.

### [MemScope: Evolving Agent Memory Architectures through Localized Code Repair](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21170)

Agent memory architectures are usually frozen once deployed, and the self-evolving approaches that adapt them rely on coarse feedback such as scores or summaries, which often triggers wholesale rewrites of a system that mostly works. MemScope instead gives a tool-using Patch Agent the raw execution traces, the runtime memory state and the architecture code, so it can pinpoint where a failure originates and make bounded, in-place edits. Treating memory design as code that can be repaired locally, rather than regenerated, keeps the update scope constrained and preserves the parts that were already doing their job.

## Agent harness, context and skills

### [Prune the Summary, Not the History: Source-Judged Compaction for Long-Horizon Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20927)

Context compaction replaces an overgrown history with a summary, but compaction prompts ask for decisions and next steps, so the summary blends facts from the environment with reasoning invented by the compressor, and the agent cannot tell which is which. In an analysis of a widely used structured compressor, 83% of failed runs traced back to an action carrying out a summary entry, most of which no environment output supported. Source-Judged Compaction is a training-free extra step that scores each entry for whether it has a source in the environment outputs or the previous summary and drops the ones that do not, at the cost of one cheap judge call per compaction.

### [Skip the Compressor: Coding Agents Fix More Issues When They Pick Whole Files](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21073)

Coding agents face repositories far larger than their context window, and the usual fixes, retrievers and prompt compressors, both fall short when a patch requires the exact lines being edited. The authors show that at an equal token budget, spending it on choosing which whole files to show verbatim beats pruning tokens or retrieving chunks. Their simple selector, CQ2-CARE, resolved 43.7% and 46.7% of 300 recent SWE-rebench issues with two frontier models, about 6.3 points above the best of three compressors. The lesson for harness builders is that fidelity of the code in context can matter more than squeezing in more of it.

### [Evolving Together, Failing Apart: The Skill-Policy Co-evolution Trap in Self-Improving Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20845)

Some self-improving agents train a policy and a skill bank together, then evaluate the policy on the final bank it grew up with. In deployment, however, skills get rewritten, added or removed far more often than the policy is retrained. The paper argues, with theory and experiments, that the shared acceptance rule ties competence to the co-evolved bank, so the policy becomes more fragile to skill drift as training proceeds. The authors introduce a drift audit that evaluates policies on altered banks, giving anyone shipping evolving skill libraries a way to check whether their agent depends on one exact version of its own instructions.

### [SkillNEO: Non-Elitist Evolutionary Optimization of Agent Skills](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20926)

Skill evolution normally keeps only versions that immediately score better, but a good fix may need several interdependent edits, so a temporarily worse version can still be the right place to keep working. SkillNEO separates the version chosen for use from the versions kept for further improvement, and compares how related versions handle the same task to reveal which steps work and which failures remain. It can also go back and revise earlier versions in light of later evidence. The authors evaluate it on 86 SkillsBench tasks across several models, treating skill files less like finished products and more like drafts worth patient iteration.

### [FedSkillGit: Federated Skill Evolution via Version Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21348)

When agents learn reusable natural-language skills from experience that sits with separate owners who cannot pool raw data, the obvious design lets each client rewrite a whole skill document and send it for aggregation. The authors point out three problems: the rewritten document can leak client-specific details from private traces, nearly identical text is sent again and again, and one client's contribution gets buried where the server cannot attribute it. FedSkillGit swaps full documents for commits, in which each client submits a bounded set of patches over named rules, so skill sharing starts to look like a pull-request workflow.

### [What Survives Self-Evolution: Provenance Marks in Copied Agent Harnesses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21458)

Agent harnesses, the prompts and tool code wrapped around a base model, leak: the paper notes that Claude Code's roughly 512,000 lines were mirrored within hours of a packaging error. A plagiarist could run a leaked harness through a published self-evolver and ship the result as original work. The authors measure whether provenance marks survive that rewriting and find that published ones largely do not: a prompt watermark survived in none of 12 copies under GEPA, and AgentMark in 5 of 12. The proposer model's habits mattered enormously, so they design a new mark keyed to a table of object locations that is harder to rewrite away.

### [Improving Environment-Agnostic Coding Agents: The Role of Scaffolding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22289)

As models get better at inventing their own procedures, developer-written scaffolding might become unnecessary, yet it still pays off. The authors run a Codex-based agent with unchanged code and general tools across ARC-AGI-3, AGI Maze and Terminal-Bench, and show that merely changing one instruction, to report difficulties and hand control back for automatic continuation, lifts mean scores on the first two. Even so, the general agent trails an ARC-AGI-3-specific reference harness, and what that reference supplies is organization and control rather than game rules or solutions, which hints at where scaffolding still earns its keep.

## AI security

### [INFUSE: A Unified Surface Benchmark for Indirect Prompt Injection in Agentic Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20776)

Most prompt-injection tests drop a malicious string into an initial prompt or a single tool call, whereas real agents run shell commands, edit code, install dependencies and talk to remote services over long workflows. INFUSE covers that lifecycle with 40 realistic tasks and a taxonomy of 56 attack scenarios in five threat categories, injected through the surfaces an agent actually meets mid-task. The authors evaluate 19 models from the GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Gemma and Qwen families, producing 41,824 adversarial trajectories, a scale that should make comparisons of defenses more convincing than one-off demonstrations.

### [Language Is a Security Surface for Agent Guards](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22244)

Step-level guards decide before every tool call whether an agent's next action is safe, but the guard prompts and the benchmarks used to train and test them are in English while users are not. LinguaGuardBench translates only the user instruction into German, French, Turkish, Swahili and Amharic, leaving every tool call, observation and label identical, so any change in verdict is due to language alone. Across 19 guards, most open, small and purpose-built ones change verdicts under translation beyond their own noise, and the effect grows as the language gets rarer, which makes language a cheap way to probe a guard's blind spots.

### [My Credentials, Please: Detecting Filesystem Capability Laundering in MCP Servers at Scale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22301)

In typical local deployments, approving an MCP tool call gives the server process broad access to the host filesystem rather than limiting it to the declared operation. The authors call the resulting weakness filesystem capability laundering: attacker-steered arguments make a tool read sensitive files far outside its stated contract. Their MCP-FSD pipeline infers least-privilege boundaries per tool, plants credential fixtures outside them and tests exploitability dynamically. Of 21,097 servers from nine registries, 787 of the 4,806 triaged ones, 16.4%, showed filesystem capabilities despite no declared need, a sobering measure of how loose the MCP ecosystem's contracts are.

### [Approve What, Exactly? Effect Binding and Trusted Rendering for Agent Step-Up Authorization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063)

Agents increasingly pause for a human approval before sending money, deleting files or emailing outsiders, and that approval is treated like a second factor. The authors argue it is one only if a compromised agent cannot produce it and it names exactly what is approved. They define twelve attacks on the approval path, among them decoy recipients, amount cancellation and threshold splitting, and run them against AP2, Microsoft's action-bound design and MCP elicitation. Of ten audited runtimes, none offered a third-party verifiable approval record, and four of eight with measurable binding allowed arguments to change after approval. Their reference gateway renders the approval card from a canonical effect and has a phone passkey sign it.

### [Shortcut Sensitivity in Agent-Safety Evaluations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22243)

If the data used to evaluate agent guards contains cues that make labels predictable, a guard can score well without really measuring safety. The authors build a three-level auditing framework and apply it to LinuxArena and two benchmarks derived from TS-Bench, finding for instance that shorter shell commands strongly predict trajectory labels in LinuxArena. A released guard's F1 drops from .902 to .719 on AgentHarm-Traj when interactions are weighted equally rather than by row, while balanced accuracy actually rises. Their reading is that today's guard leaderboards may owe more to dataset artifacts than to judgment.

### [Poisoning the Neighborhood: A Topology-Aware Topic-Level Attack on Large-Scale HNSW-based RAG Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21464)

Earlier RAG poisoning attacks assume exact retrieval, in which poisoned documents compete in a global ranking, but deployed systems use approximate nearest-neighbor indexes such as HNSW, where limited search budgets and graph reachability blunt those attacks. This paper adapts by arranging poisoned documents in a hub-and-satellite structure: satellites supply competitive entry points across semantic regions, while hubs reinforce local cohesion so retrieval cascades through the poisoned neighborhood. A companion generation attack pairs evidence anchoring with entity-claim coupling to promote a chosen claim, and the whole approach is designed to generalize to unseen queries on a topic.

## Engineering blogs

### [Simon Willison: Quoting Addy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt, and Qing Wang](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/16/code-review/)

Simon Willison highlights a passage from a write-up on how the Claude Code team approaches code review. The team swapped the question of whether a human read the code for a sharper one: what could go wrong with this change, and which check, automated or human, is best placed to catch it. That reframing also moved review in time, from a gate at the end of the road, where a colleague reads a pull request, to something closer to spellcheck that runs continuously while code is written. Most changes have already been reviewed and fixed by Claude at least once before a person is asked to look, saving human attention for higher-level questions.

### [Simon Willison: LaTeX renderer](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/15/latex/)

Willison's latest small tool renders LaTeX from the URL fragment, inspired by Tiny Little Office, a suite of web editors from Paul Ford and Aboard Foundary that keeps each document in the fragment so every file is a shareable link. That app also ships an llms.txt telling agents how to construct such URLs, and Claude used it to generate a deck explaining the format. Willison had Claude Code build the LaTeX version from a short sequence of prompts, with a custom compression dictionary for LaTeX, and then asked it to borrow the iframe sandbox technique from his SVG-in-Markdown tool as protection against XSS.
