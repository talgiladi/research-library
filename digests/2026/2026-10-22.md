### [Sam Altman's three endings for the AI safety race](https://therundownai.beehiiv.com/p/sam-altman-three-endings-for-the-ai-safety-race)
According to The Information, as relayed by The Rundown, Sam Altman gave OpenAI staff an internal talk last week sketching three endgames for safe AI. His favored option is a global enforcer modeled on the International Atomic Energy Agency, something OpenAI has lobbied for since 2023. The interesting part is the deadline: OpenAI's current ship-once-it-passes-tests approach is "pacing 1.0", and Altman wants "pacing 2.0" chosen within a year, including an explicit speed limit or stopping point for self-improving AI. ### [Claude designs protein binders against scorpion neurotoxins](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-scorpion-toxin-binder-design)
Anthropic and the investment-research firm Citrini set out to test whether motivated non-experts with Claude could tackle a neglected disease, and picked scorpion stings, which hit more than 1.2 million people a year and kill over 3,250, mostly children. ### [MemCurrent: designing persistent memory for production agents](https://www.databricks.com/blog/memcurrent-designing-persistent-memory-production-agents)
Databricks' MemCurrent splits agent memory into append-only episodic traces of every session and a mutable "derived" knowledge base of distilled lessons, topped by an auto-generated memory.md index injected at session start. Agents write explicit memories through tools, while an offline "dreaming" pipeline distills implicit lessons, merges duplicates and resolves conflicts in the background. On an internal benchmark built from 114 real Genie tasks, pass rate went from 22.3% with no memory to 35.8% with episodic only, 66.1% with derived only and 68.4% with both, closing over 80% of the gap to gold-memory injection while running 1.35× faster and 1.44× cheaper. On LoCoMo, LongMemEval and ConvoMem it matched full context at about 12× fewer tokens, ahead of Mem0, PropMem and OpenClaw memory. It ships in Genie and the Agent Bricks Memory beta.

### [How Kiro fixes itself, and how we keep the fixes honest](https://kiro.dev/blog/how-kiro-fixes-itself/)
Amazon's Kiro team built kiro-trace to mine its coding agent's own runs for failures, and the founding example is sobering: one false sentence in a tool description reached the model 61,742 times and nobody would ever have filed a ticket. The loop proposes one change at a time, but the rule is that the part proposing a change never decides whether it worked; a deterministic three-stage gate compares each change against a re-run control, checks for regressions and throws out runs where the agent looked up answers online, and a human merges. Week one produced eight PRs and seven merged, fixing a silent search bug that broke one search in eight and cutting redundant re-reads by 83%. A slimmer tool profile scoring 82.4% versus 77.5% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 at a third of the tokens is still under product review.

### [Can AI free up scientists to think bigger?](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/can-ai-free-up-scientists-to-think-bigger)
An MIT CSAIL paper, "AI Disruption and Falling Costs in Theory Research," tests models released between December 2024 and April 2026 on open theory problems from 254 recent arXiv papers in mathematics, physics, economics and four other fields, with human answers the models had never seen. The share of problems on which models reached the authors' own conclusion climbed from 3% with o1 to 35% with GPT-5.5, roughly 22 points a year, at about $5 per attempt or $15 per success, down from around $170 per full-marks answer. That works out to under 0.3% of an expert's cost. The authors argue the bottleneck shifts to verification and a growing backlog of untested hypotheses, so the field will reorganize around those tasks rather than lose its scientists.

### [DeepInfer: DeepSeek open-sources its production inference engine](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepInfer)

### [Introducing TorchTPU: a native PyTorch backend for TPUs](https://pytorch.org/blog/introducing-torchtpu/)
Google and Meta's PyTorch team have open-sourced TorchTPU, a backend started in mid-2025 that treats PyTorch as a first-class citizen on TPUs rather than squeezing it into JAX's way of working, as PyTorch/XLA and TorchAX did. The goal is that porting a workload means changing `.to("cuda")` to `.to("tpu")`, with no JAX dependency in the runtime, a solid eager mode and torch.compile built on Dynamo instead of lazy tensors. More importantly, Google says it is already in production for training and inference at some of its largest Cloud customers.

### [The Age of Reasoning (Metis Reasoning whitepaper)](https://metisreasoning.ai/whitepaper/the-age-of-reasoning-2026-10.pdf)

### [Making every harness durable](https://vercel.com/blog/making-every-harness-durable)
Coding-agent harnesses were built for laptops, where the session lives in memory and resuming takes a prompt, but in the cloud a function timeout or dropped connection mid-turn means lost or repeated work. Vercel's open-source durable-harness wraps Pi, OpenCode, Codex and Claude Code in the Workflow SDK's durable backend, which can also be self-hosted on Postgres. Before each model or tool call it records an intent in a log and afterward the outcome, and a queue message backstops the turn so a new function can pick up where a dead one stopped. Finished model calls are never billed twice, tools marked replay-safe rerun, and anything else returns to the model as "possibly run" so it can check, while Claude Code transcripts and Codex rollouts are mirrored for rebuilds.

### [Uber's AI infra from the inside](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ubers-ai-infra-from-the-inside)

### [LLM prompt caching versus semantic caching](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/llm-prompt-caching-versus-semantic)
ByteByteGo (often partly paywalled; this one was readable) separates two caches teams tend to blur. Prompt caching reuses the model's key-value states for an exact shared prefix, so stable instructions, manuals and tool definitions must come first. Even a timestamp at the top breaks reuse. With Anthropic's pricing, one write plus one read costs $1.35 instead of $2, trimming input cost and time to first token but never output. Semantic caching lives in the app and returns a stored answer for a similar question, which skips the model entirely but fails on near-misses such as cancelling before versus after shipping, negation, plan tiers and dates. A 0.92 similarity is not 92% correctness. The recommended stack is an exact cache, then a semantic cache, then the model with prompt caching, and cached answers must never stand in for actions.

### [arXiv's updated rate-limit policy](https://blog.arxiv.org/2026/10/01/updated-rate-limit-policy/)
arXiv, the source of half this digest, received 40,363 submissions this September, double September 2024 and four times 2016, generating almost 9,000 support tickets for staff and volunteer moderators. Moderators are seeing more thin, narrow papers, "salami" papers cut from a single work and dense AI-written submissions, with a small group of authors eating a disproportionate share of moderation time and delaying everyone else's papers by days or weeks. As a stopgap since October 1, every submitter is limited to two submissions per calendar month and three active submissions at once while arXiv works out best practice for AI-assisted authorship. The policy still allows disclosed AI use, as long as the work meets arXiv's scholarly standards.

### [Beyond g-mean²: comparing monitorability and deployment risk](https://alignment.openai.com/beyond-gmean-squared/)
OpenAI's alignment team argues that its own g-mean² score for chain-of-thought monitorability can mislead, because different models produce misbehavior on different subsets of tasks and because a single score ignores how monitors are actually used. It adds two metrics. A pair-matched score compares models only on instances both support and combines them with variance-weighted HodgeRank into an ordering. A post-control score simulates a monitor rejecting outputs, as Codex Auto-review does, and estimates how often a bad output still gets through when the model may retry. That matters because a model trained against a monitor can misbehave less overall yet slip more accepted hacks past it. Applied to system-card data from o1 through GPT-5.6 Sol, the matched and dataset-level orderings mostly agreed, with disagreements marking near-ties.

## Agent memory and context

### [More Memory, Worse Agents: Error Reproduction and Anti-Persistence in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27406)
Self-improving agents usually store not just their past trajectories but the rules, summaries and skills they infer from them, and this paper identifies a quiet failure mode in that habit. A wrong abstraction gets retrieved and reused, steers new trajectories, and those trajectories become the raw material for fresh abstractions that reproduce the original mistake, a cycle the authors call error reproduction. Their fix, Anti-Persistence, keeps accumulating trajectories and outcomes but never stores guidance as an object: an adaptive selector uses past outcomes to decide whether and how to synthesize task-specific advice on demand, then throws it away after execution. Across benchmarks and backbones it posts the best held-out success and the lowest average LLM-call cost of the memory designs tested.

### [MemDev-Bench: Does Memory Help Code Agents? Benchmarking Memory Systems over Real Repository Histories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27524)
Code agents increasingly work in the same repository for weeks, so MemDev-Bench asks whether memory actually helps them. The benchmark pairs 555 base tasks with 4,869 earlier tasks from the same repositories, each made executable and annotated for how relevant it is, so history can be controlled. Memory does improve fine-grained localization and editing, but it also breaks tasks the agent could otherwise solve, even when the history is related, and unrelated history makes this worse, mostly because agents reuse memories without checking whether they still apply. The surprise is that handing agents raw past trajectories cuts their step counts far more than any memory system, since compressed memories drop the process details agents actually reuse.

### [REM: What to Store, When to Recall, What to Forget in Experience Memory for Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27377)
REM argues that experience reuse fails less because of retrieval than because nobody governs a memory's lifecycle from inside the agent's loop. Each finished trajectory is consolidated into a phase-structured document about a hundred times smaller than the original. That document is injected at task start, while the costlier implementation record is held back until an in-loop gate notices the agent struggling, and then evicted after a single exposure. On SWE-ContextBench Lite, REM retrieves the right past trajectory 68.7% of the time versus 55.6% for the best prior system, and lifts resolution from 24.9% to 27.6% at 17% lower cost in every replicate. Injecting the same experience as a summary or raw trajectory, even with oracle retrieval, does not help.

### [Memory as Code: Declarative Retrieval over Codified Long-Term Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27700)
When facts are scattered across sessions and their relationships change over time, ranking memories by relevance struggles to compose them into an answer. Memory as Code treats memory the way a programmer treats a codebase. Construction turns interaction history into a three-layer executable structure, running from atomic functions through a relational layer to modular compositions, and retrieval becomes declarative: the query is translated into a structured retrieval program that runs over that memory instead of pulling the top-k similar facts. Because the same code abstraction shapes both how memory is built and how it is queried, the two are designed together rather than bolted on. On LoCoMo and LongMemEval it beats the strongest baseline in each setting by up to 15.91% relative LLM-judge accuracy while using substantially fewer tokens.

### [Disclosure Begets Disclosure: A Self-Reinforcing Privacy Loop in LLM Agents with Persistent Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28315)
This study finds that persistent memory leaks personal data through ordinary, non-adversarial helpfulness. In multi-session simulations with a user holding 24 personal attributes, support agents that consult memory give better answers but let an observer infer more attributes from the accumulated record, by a margin that widens each session. The mechanism is a loop: memory-conditioned replies carry earlier values forward as presuppositions, users confirm or ignore them, and the agent's own guesses become recorded facts. Users cannot escape it on their own, because correcting a value discloses it, declining shifts predictions toward sensitive values and uncorrected errors become entrenched. The effective fix sits at the agent's response: telling it not to state or presuppose attributes brings leakage near no-memory levels while keeping most of the quality gain.

### [Evaluating Context Compaction on the Questions That Come Later](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27301)
Agents compact their context for the question in front of them, but the compacted text also has to serve whatever comes next, and that is where this evaluation finds the problem. Across four corpora with evidence annotated for several questions per text, letting gpt-6-luna keep only the sentences needed for the current MultiRC question raises accuracy on that question by 11.4 points at a quarter of the tokens, yet on later questions no sentence-selection method beats deleting sentences at random. Question-agnostic word-level compression with LLMLingua-2 beats random deletion by 12.5 points on those later questions, though the ranking flips on τ-bench trajectories, where later tool calls need exact identifiers. The lesson is that a compaction's score on its own question says little about the next one.

### [Cache Engineering for Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28577)
As agent trajectories grow, KV-cache I/O and storage, not tokens, become the dominant cost, and the usual context-engineering tricks of trimming or summarizing history make things worse by changing the prefix and forcing costly re-prefills. The authors propose cache engineering instead: agents directly drop selected cache entries from future attention and reposition retained keys' position encodings, without touching the trajectory text. Because identical tokens can then map to different cache states, plain prefix matching breaks, so they co-design AKV, a serving engine with a stateless, API-compatible interface that manages stateful reuse and restoration internally. A rolling-drop policy for tool responses beats threshold-triggered summarization by 1.7 benchmark points at 27.7% lower cost, with up to 1.2× throughput, and self-improved policies save another 7.1%.

## Agent harness and self-improvement

### [Elicitation or Expansion? Demystifying Gains from Agent Harness Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27889)
Harness optimization has been posting big wins without touching model weights, but this paper asks what kind of win it is. Borrowing the pass@k analysis used to tell whether RL training teaches models anything new, the authors separate elicitation, meaning more reliable success on tasks the agent could already reach, from expansion, meaning a broader set of solvable tasks. The distinction matters for recursive self-improvement, because only expansion feeds the loop new successful experience. Comparing optimized harnesses with their originals, with model scaling and with extra inference compute, they find that a harness's pass@1 advantage over those stronger interventions can reverse at larger attempt budgets, and that aggregate gains hide shortfalls on the hardest tasks, so a single success rate is the wrong yardstick.

### [Harness Self-Evolution for Long-Horizon Terminal Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27435)
Terminal agents often solve a task in one run and fail it in the next, and RiseHarness focuses on exactly those flip-flopping tasks to make an existing model's capability reliable without touching its weights. Successful runs serve as references for diagnosing failed ones, combining agent trajectories with internal harness traces from a runtime monitor to see how actions interact with command execution, feedback, context management and the decision to stop. A Harness Handbook maps that evidence onto harness behaviors and source code, and the system then searches other open harness frameworks for relevant mechanisms and adapts them to the current model. Candidates go through behavioral checks and repeated evaluation, and only measured gains seed the next round, gradually merging ideas from several harnesses into one.

### [Recoverability and Verifier Strength: Two Measured Preconditions for Unattended Agent Self-Improvement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27465)
With platforms starting to read deployed agents' traces, derive changes and ship them without a human, this paper proposes two things to measure before letting that loop run unattended. Verifier strength is how well the automated judge agrees with a trusted reference, and recoverability is how cheaply a bad change can be undone. In practice the platform's LLM judge sometimes returned the same score whatever it was shown, in 9 of 37 settings, without reporting an error, so a verifier can look healthy while approving anything. Restoring a saved copy brought the agent back, but a regenerated undo did not, and a deployed change shifted 17% of task outcomes beyond normal noise. The resulting rule lets prompt, tool and skill edits run unattended while weight updates still need sign-off.

### [Training the Whole Agent, Not Just the Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28069)
RL for coding agents normally updates the policy while the harness stays frozen, even though trajectories keep exposing flawed repair strategies and interaction problems that shift as the policy learns. HarnessAdapt-RL adapts both together. A frozen teacher writes task-specific, mechanism-level skills for bad repair strategies, while a second teacher evolves tools and hooks for recurring interaction problems, keeping a change only if it improves the observed pass or trainable rate without lowering either. With a 9B base model, pass@1 rises over GRPO by 9.8, 12.6 and 6.4 points on SWE-bench Verified, Multilingual and Pro, beating GRPO with the hand-built OpenHands harness. Skill gains persist after the skills are removed, but stripping the evolved tools and hooks costs up to 7.7 points, so training really produces a model-harness pair.

### [Object-Oriented Harness: Evolving Agent Harnesses through Object-Oriented Design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28380)
A single global harness struggles when tasks need very different execution strategies, so OO-Harness borrows object-oriented design to evolve a family of them. During training, an evolution agent reads execution records and creates specialized harnesses as subclasses of the base harness or of earlier specialized ones, inheriting what works and overriding prompts, tools or control flow where a task type needs something different. At test time, a router uses routing lessons accumulated during training to pick which class should run each task. Across three long-horizon benchmarks covering business workflows, workspace tasks and tool use, the approach beats the compared optimization methods, with up to a 15% absolute improvement over the base harness, and ablations break down the contribution of inheritance and routing.

### [Evolving Agent Interfaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28601)
Computer-use agents are stuck with interfaces built for humans, typing a spreadsheet cell by cell when one line of code would do or printing two thousand lines to find a function. Agent S4 treats interface design as something the agent should learn: a meta-agent studies task agents' trajectories on synthetic tasks, without success labels, and builds and revises a repository of interfaces and knowledge about when to use them, while population-based evolution keeps repositories that perform well on unseen validation tasks. With Qwen3.8-27B, it sets an open-source state of the art on OSWorld V2 at a 21.3% binary score, 3.8 times the same model with fixed interfaces and above both GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8, at a small fraction of their cost. ScienceBoard success rises from 41.4% to 58.6%.

### [RITE: Evaluating End-to-End Resilience to Silent Tool Errors in E-Commerce Agentic Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27777)
The nastiest tool failures do not throw errors: a tool's expected output drifts between development and execution, returning a plausible but wrong value that flows quietly into the final answer. RITE, built from human-authored industrial e-commerce workflows and production-log inputs, measures whether agents still complete the original query after such a silent error corrupts an intermediate state. Errors are injected into fixed workflow prefixes using a semantic taxonomy, recorded tool behavior is frozen and agents then continue freely, yielding 501 instances across nine workflows. Across 10 recent LLMs, silent errors roughly halve task success and are consistently harder than explicit failures, with noticing the error, using an available verification path and acting on the evidence forming successive bottlenecks. A lightweight State Checker is offered as a first mitigation.

## LLM and agent security

### [ClawMMU: Protection-Coherent Virtual Memory for Stateful LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28093)
Much of what an agent remembers comes from web pages and tool results, yet harnesses record nothing about who may act on that memory, which is why an instruction hidden in a fetched page can end up treated as the user's own. ClawMMU borrows from operating systems and puts a protection layer around agent memory, labeling every piece with its owner, scope, origins and authority. Those labels are checked whenever content enters the model's context, is written to storage, comes back out or drives a tool call. The central rule is that information may rise in authority but authority never lifts itself, and because checks depend on provenance rather than content, memory-poisoning attacks become ordinary protection faults the harness blocks even when the model itself is fooled.

### [Act on Your Behalf, Beyond Your Authorization: Benchmarking LLM Agent Authorization Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27548)
Most agent evaluations ask whether a task got done or something harmful happened, which misses agents that quietly exceed what the user actually delegated. AuthorizationBench builds 286 executable tasks from documented real-world cases in ten domains, run in a sandbox where both authorized and out-of-scope paths remain observable, and collects 6,864 trajectories from four models, two reasoning settings and three harnesses. Agents step outside their authorization in 19.2% of runs, ranging from 10.5% to 26.6% across configurations, and more reasoning effort barely changes that. They overstep about as often whether or not there is direct harm, and more under benign pressure than under malicious injection, 23.5% versus 17.6%. Agents that check in with the user before finishing overstep less, 14.0% versus 21.6%.

### [Local Allow, Global Deny: Why Per-Action Authorization Does Not Compose in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27620)
Most agent guardrails screen each tool call on its own, and this paper proves that is structurally insufficient. A compositional authorization failure is a single execution or a pair of executions in which every action passes the local guard, yet together they violate a global policy about intent, purpose, provenance, consent or delegation. The central result is an impossibility: relational purpose violations have no single trace to blame, so a per-action monitor that never raises false alarms catches none of them, regardless of the model behind it. A SAT/SMT synthesizer called AL-SYNTH generates minimal witnesses, and nine LLM judges approve them almost every time when shown one action, but flag them when shown both executions together. None of the six surveyed guardrails monitors across executions.

### [The Show-Up Attack: When Subagents Win Over Their Supervisor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27574)
In multi-agent systems the supervisor decides who works on what, and this paper shows a malicious subagent can talk its way into tasks far outside its assigned scope, which the authors call a show-up attack. Once involved, the subagent gains access to work and data it was never meant to touch. To measure the risk they build SUA-Arena, with six attack strategies and 188 test cases, and test six frontier LLMs as supervisors. All of them prove vulnerable: GPT-5.6-Sol in the supervisor seat reaches an attack success rate as high as 0.93. The obvious mitigations, defensive prompting and restricting how agents communicate, do not cut the risk consistently, which leads the authors to argue that orchestration frameworks need explicit, enforced authority boundaries.

### [After the Attacker Leaves: Benchmarking Carryover Attacks on LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28425)
Modern assistants persist state in two ways, memory files silently prepended to the system prompt and scheduled tasks that wake the agent without user input, and a single indirect prompt injection planted in either can keep firing long after the attacker is gone. Existing benchmarks only score attacks in the session where the malicious content first appears, so CarryoverBench separates planting from triggering across 12 personal-assistant scenarios and 101 attack tasks, covering same-turn, heartbeat-scheduled and memory-resurfaced channels. Across five frontier models, planted payloads re-fire with no measurable decay over five rounds, and once a payload is planted the trigger stage adds little protection. Porting the memory channel to the Hermes runtime weakens but does not remove the effect, pointing to runtime-layer controls rather than prompt fixes.

### [AutoRedClaw: Automated Red-Teaming for Claw-Style Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27244)
Persistent "claw-style" assistants such as OpenClaw reload their configuration files, long-term memory and skills into context every session, so one poisoning becomes a standing threat, and hand-built test suites can't keep up with how fast these agents change. AutoRedClaw generates end-to-end attacks together with the environments needed to run them, organized by risk category, strategy, stage and attack surface, with experience-guided exploration steering toward under-tested regions. Strict quality checks and execution-based validation keep samples realistic, and the effective attack rate tops 30% at under $0.10 per successful sample. The resulting 330-case RedClaw-Bench yields far higher attack success than AgentDojo, AgentDyn or DTap, and most of the seven harnesses tested with four frontier models exceed 50% attack success.

### [Toolflation: Seller Agents Distort Tool Markets Through Spontaneous Generative Engine Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28454)
As agents begin selling tools to other agents, this paper finds that seller agents told only to get picked more often start gaming the listings on their own. In markets built from real Amazon and Instagram data tools, where every tool is run on every question, sellers under all five production models rewrite descriptions to claim ever more fields, including ones their tools return for fewer than half the targets. Often that shifts choices away from the most capable tool, and under one model sellers copy each other until listings are indistinguishable. Buyers then pick by list position, near-useless tools win a quarter to two fifths of choices, and task success falls from 89% to about 30% on Amazon. The authors argue honesty toward third parties belongs in safety testing before models act as sellers.
