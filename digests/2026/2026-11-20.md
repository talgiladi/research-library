## Models and the industry

### [Gemini 4 Neon: Google's mid-tier model lands near the top](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)

Almost every newsletter in the inbox led with Google, and for good reason: Gemini 4 Neon is the company's fourth Gemini 4 model in under two months, and despite its mid-tier label it scores 61 on Artificial Analysis' index, edging past Google's own top-end Carbon and trailing only Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.2 Astra. Google's own testing has it beating Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.2 Sol on 16 of 19 benchmarks, with the widest leads in long-document work, office automation and cyber tasks, and [The Deep View](https://www.thedeepview.com/newsletter/newsom-pitches-california-ai-rules-as-us-template) adds that it costs 32% less per task than Carbon and tests as more resistant to prompt injection than the Anthropic and OpenAI models on Gray Swan's benchmark. The catch is price and naming: at $3/$15 per million tokens it sits between Sol and Opus, and [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/18/llm-gemini/), shipping Neon support in llm-gemini 0.38, points out that while Carbon remains half-price until 1 December, Neon is actually the most expensive model in the lineup, and Google's launch graphic quietly pretends Argon does not exist. ### [OpenAI's research-acceleration report: agents now finish nearly half of 2–4 hour research tasks](https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-jagged-frontier/)

Picked up by TLDR AI, OpenAI's second report on how coding agents are speeding up its own research is one of the more concrete recursive-self-improvement disclosures any lab has published. Between March and October, estimated autonomous success rose from 23% to 46% on tasks that take a human two to four hours, and from 13% to 35% on four-to-eight-hour tasks, but the frontier is jagged: agents succeed 79% of the time at monitoring training runs, yet only 35% at launching them and 25% at writing inference and serving code. Usage is exploding, with the median researcher burning $2,400 a day at list prices in October and the 90th percentile at $23,000, and in a survey researchers said they would give up 30% of team compute or 40% of headcount rather than lose frontier coding agents for three months. More importantly for the safety debate, documented Safety and Alignment workweeks grew 77% from January to September, accelerating after the July Hugging Face incident, and OpenAI repeats its call for labs to be required to report RSI indicators publicly.

## Agent harnesses and coding agents

### [Inside the Codex sandbox](https://openai.com/index/inside-the-codex-sandbox/)

OpenAI's Michael Bolin, in a post TLDR AI linked, explains how Codex runs commands on your own machine within filesystem and network limits set by "permission profiles." The default workspace profile allows writes inside the repo while keeping `.git` and `.codex` read-only, so the agent cannot plant Git hooks or edit its own config, allows broad reads because compilers and SDKs live outside the repo, and blocks network access to stop exfiltration. The candid part is the threat model: the post admits sensitive files elsewhere stay readable and gives a concrete escape, an agent tampering with a git-ignored copy of Prettier in `node_modules` that your editor then runs outside the sandbox on save. Network rules are enforced by a proxy running outside the sandbox, the same API is implemented on macOS, Linux and Windows, and a `codex sandbox` command exposes it for your own tools.

### [Why Antigravity eats your weekly quota, and what fixes it](https://asubedi.com.np/blog/why-antigravity-eats-weekly-quota-fast/)

This TLDR pick is a satisfying piece of reverse engineering. Ashish Subedi suspected that Google Antigravity burns quota because Gemini's implicit prompt cache expires during the natural pauses of coding, so he disassembled the 193 MB Go agent binary, found telemetry buckets split at exactly five minutes, and binary-searched the eviction cliff down to a 15-second window. He also discovered a dormant keep-alive engine Google already wrote, `keepalive.go`, which pings with a one-token request at 4m30s; once its feature flags were unlocked it kept caches warm across six-minute pauses. His practical findings: a capped keep-alive of about three pings saves 30% to 88% of effective tokens across a pause, and even a five-word prompt starts with 13,800 to 16,800 tokens of system prompt, MCP schemas and skills, so disabling unused MCP servers and plugins shrinks every cold turn.

### [Switching LLMs mid-conversation is a ski-rental problem](https://www.edgee.ai/blog/posts/switching-llms-mid-conversation-is-a-ski-rental-problem)

Most model routers ask which model should handle a request, but Edgee's Khaled Maâmra points out that for coding agents that is only half the question, because every switch throws away the prompt cache and forces the new model to reread the whole conversation cold. On Anthropic, writing a cache costs 1.25 times the input price and reading it a tenth, so a single rewrite on a long session can wipe out many turns of savings from a cheaper model. The post frames when to switch as the classic ski-rental problem, with a simple rule and a worst-case guarantee: count the savings you have given up by staying, and move once they match the cost of moving. Edgee's gateway pairs this with a four-tier difficulty classifier and, weekly, replays 30 days of an organization's sessions under every candidate tier map and switching setting to recommend the cheapest one that keeps each turn on a capable model.

### [Ship Agent Changes Safely in 5 Steps](https://www.decodingai.com/p/ship-agent-changes-safely)

Paul Iusztin's latest Decoding AI issue goes after the quiet fear of changing a prompt that seemed fine and not knowing what you broke, and the answer is to build evals from your own failures, label traces, and replay every failure before shipping. Only the first of the five steps is public: rather than writing elaborate eval criteria up front, pull 20 to 50 real traces, have one domain expert mark each Pass or Fail with a short critique of the first thing that went wrong, then cluster the failures and rank them by frequency and severity, on the premise that the dataset, not the system prompt, is the real asset. The rest, which builds toward replaying labeled traces as a regression suite, is behind the paywall.

### [Agentic Harness Engineering Beta: Requirement-Centered Evolution of Agent Harnesses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24924)

Automatic harness-evolution methods usually let one agent patch the harness and keep whatever raises a benchmark score, which the authors argue produces narrow fixes and benchmark-specific local optima. AHE-Beta borrows real engineering practice instead: simulated users work with the harness over multiple turns and file their needs as issues, a team of agents implements them as general changes rather than task-specific patches, and a change is merged only if it resolves a verified requirement and passes review. On held-out tasks, pass@1 rises from 21.9% to 33.3% with 40.8% fewer tokens than the base harness, beating Claude Code, Meta-Harness and the earlier AHE, and real users preferred it in 31 of 48 blind pairs.

### [Thinking Fast, Anywhere: One System-1 for the Decision Points of Any Agent Workflow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23751)

Agents call a big model at every step even when many steps are just picking among a few known options, and existing fast/slow agent designs need hand-written hooks for each environment. AnyS1's insight is that procedural skills already say where choices arise and which options apply, so the authors mined enumerable decisions from more than 1,000 public skills, gathered real execution states in isolated containers, and trained a single calibrated 4B "pointer" model to resolve them. At run time routine decisions take one forward pass and fall back to the large model only when confidence drops below a threshold, which cut episode cost by 75 to 91% and model-call time by 63 to 92% on three unseen benchmarks while preserving reliability.

### [Reducing Inference Cost of Coding Agents with Timeout Cascades](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24324)

After evaluating 36 open-weight models released since 2024 on a software-engineering benchmark, the authors find that small models are closing the gap with large ones, and that what gap remains is concentrated in hard tasks while small models do remarkably well on easy and medium ones. That suggests a very simple cascade: try the small model first and escalate to the large one only when it times out or fails. On SWE-bench Verified this cut total GPU-hour cost by up to 33% without losing accuracy, and it nearly matched an oracle task router, which makes it a cheap alternative to training a learned router.

### [Where the Tokens Go: Heat, Entropy and Error in Agentic Coding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23375)

This is the most unusual paper of the day, a "software thermodynamics" that splits an agent's token spend into work and heat, with the test suite acting as a Maxwell's daemon judging each change. To measure it, the author has an agent make a change and then undo it many times over, an analogue of single-molecule pulling experiments, and defines a software temperature from the spread in cost, which rises with the model's sampling temperature. The headline numbers are striking: about 80% of the tokens in a successful run are heat, generated in calls that leave the requested change where it was, and the estimated free energy of a change is only 14 to 22% of the mean cost of a successful run.

### [Repository-Grounded Rubrics for Execution-Free Evaluation of Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23309)

Tests can be missing, incomplete or impossible to run without a working environment, so this paper scores patches without executing anything. An agent explores the repository before the change and writes weighted pass/fail criteria for the task, then a tool-less judge scores a diff by the weighted share it satisfies. Validated on 7,291 labeled SWE-bench Verified patches from fifteen systems, the rubric score separates resolved from unresolved patches with a mean per-task AUROC of 0.826, and ranking systems by their failing patches agrees closely with ranking by resolve rate (Spearman 0.968); disagreements often trace back to tests that do not actually match the task.

### [When Single Agent Is Enough: Rethinking Multi-Agent Systems for Automated Code Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23283)

Multi-agent setups, with specialists for different quality dimensions, currently hold state-of-the-art results in review-comment generation and code refinement, but they add architectural complexity and runtime overhead. Comparing the two designs systematically, the authors find that single-agent approaches perform comparably and sometimes better, a useful data point for anyone defaulting to an orchestra of reviewers when one well-prompted agent would do.

### [When Code Works but the License Doesn't: Evaluating License Constraint Handling in Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23299)

Coding agents now choose dependencies, vendor code and prepare releases, yet evaluations only check whether the software works. This study tests whether agents building proprietary software notice and respect license constraints across permissive and copyleft licenses, missing license information, transitive dependencies, dual licensing, vendoring and distribution. The result is a persistent gap: agents happily build and test software while overlooking provenance, authorization and distribution obligations, and they do better only when licensing information turns into concrete engineering actions such as substituting a dependency or gating a release.

### [Who Changed the AI Agent's Rules? How AI Coding Agents Escalate Authority in CI/CD Pipelines](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23251)

Coding agents do not only change application code; they also edit workflows and permission settings, which quietly widens their own authority over the whole pipeline. Analyzing all 932,791 agent-authored pull requests from five autonomous agents in the AIDev dataset, the authors classify each as data-plane or control-plane and score it with a joint cost-and-governance penalty. Control-plane changes carry 3.9 times the governance risk, all 775 extreme-risk PRs touch the control plane, and policy-violation flags appear in 46.6% of control-plane PRs and, worryingly, in 63.6% of the ones that were merged.

### [automodel: A Skill for Agentic Model Discovery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24347)

Rather than building another domain-specific discovery pipeline in the AlphaEvolve mould, the authors package a "model discovery recipe" as a portable agent skill and hand it to off-the-shelf agents such as Claude Code and Codex. In case studies spanning astrophysics, cardiovascular dynamics, precision agriculture, traffic flow and tire dynamics, the equipped agents identified novel, scientifically relevant models, which is a neat demonstration of skills as a way to transfer methodology rather than code. The skill is published openly.

## Agent memory and context

### [Semantic Context Handoff with On-Demand Evidence for Long-Horizon Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24357)

Compaction schemes keep agents' context small by preserving conclusions and dropping old observations, but they throw away evidence that may matter later. Semantic Context Handoff separates the two: a compact semantic state carries the agent's current interpretation, while the underlying action-observation evidence stays addressable and can re-enter context when it becomes relevant, without extra summarization or retrieval-model calls. In a controlled 150-task SWE-bench study, evidence recovery lifts Pass@1 from 80.0% to 86.9% at essentially the same token use, and against the strongest baseline it reaches that score with 43% fewer tokens and 41% less wall-clock time.

### [ARISE: Practical Self-Evolving Memory for Accurate and Cost-Efficient Enterprise Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23588)

ARISE lets an enterprise agent learn from its own failures without touching weights, tools or workflow logic. The model itself, or a stronger teacher, attributes each failure and proposes a correction, which is stored as retrievable "micro" memory and later consolidated into "macro" rules, and a rule update is accepted only if held-out accuracy rises. Across 14 enterprise workflow domains from SOP-Bench and τ²-bench with five models, the teacher version gains 10.9 points on SOP-Bench and 5.8 on τ²-bench and beats GEPA, while the self-attributing version still gains 10 points, all at roughly the same per-task cost.

### [Resolution as Attention for Efficient Agent Memory Perception](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24422)

Most memory work asks what to keep or retrieve; this paper asks how much detail each past interaction deserves. Resolution as Attention renders an agent's history as images and adjusts each one's resolution at every step according to its relevance to the next decision, so critical interactions stay sharp while the rest consume fewer visual tokens. A lightweight relevance estimator trained with teacher guidance decides the fidelity, and across tasks and backbones the method cuts token consumption by more than half without hurting performance; it also plugs in on top of existing memory managers.

## AI security

### [Securing and Auditing Long-Horizon LLM Agents: State-Transfer Auditing in the Agent Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23346)

The paper's argument is that the dangerous moments in a long-running agent are not the prompts and tool calls that traces already log but the transfers of state between working context, session history, persistent memory and the environment. A transfer can admit unchecked content such as an indirect prompt injection or an unverified memory write, rewrite state lossily, as when compaction drops a user constraint or consolidation strips a memory's source, or restore state incompletely, as when a workspace rollback leaves stale KV state on the inference server. The proposed architecture puts an audit point at each boundary, emitting a minimal hash-chained record and separating cheap deterministic checks from model-based semantic ones, so failures can be attributed and monitored across multi-step runs.

### [Intent Contracts: Taking Tools Away from Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24025)

Intent contracts flip the usual permission model: the planning model holds no tools at all. For each step it submits a short logged contract with a goal, a verbatim quote from the user and scope fields; code validates it, and a small trusted model checks it against the user's words and writes the actual tool call. Provided the validation code is correct, every executed call comes from the trusted model and the planner's influence is confined to a contract logged in advance. With gpt-6.1-terra as planner and GLM-5.3-Flash as the trusted model on AmPermBench, every object changed under contracts was named in the goal of the contract that produced it, a stronger guarantee than reviewers that only inspect the planner's commands.

### [AutoPI: Taxonomy-Guided Adaptive Red Teaming for Prompt Injection in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24313)

Adaptive red teamers are usually tuned to maximize attack success rate, but a high rate can come from reusing a few tricks that work, leaving whole families of injection untested and absent from hardening data. AutoPI instead aims for breadth: guided by PIAtlas, a taxonomy of prompt-injection techniques, an attacker model writes an injection for every sub-technique and reworks the failures using an LLM judge's feedback, black-box and without training. It slots into AgentDojo and IPI Arena in place of fixed templates and reports technique coverage alongside success rate; against Qwen3.6-35B on AgentDojo it found working injections in all six taxonomy classes and for more than 90% of techniques.

### [PIPETTE: Prompt Injection Purple-Teaming for Robust LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24362)

Making the same point from the defender's side, PIPETTE shows that RL-based red teamers such as RL-Hammer and PISmith suffer mode collapse, repeatedly rediscovering similar attacks whose extra successes add nothing when used for adversarial training. The framework rewards diversity with a dictionary-learning-based signal so the red team covers more strategies, and couples it with an on-policy adversarial training pipeline designed for indirect prompt injection, so the hardened agent resists attacks while still completing legitimate tasks.

### [What Survives the Shift from Text to Pixels? Prompt Injection in Image-Rendered Documents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24231)

Rendering untrusted documents as page images is sometimes suggested as a cheap prompt-injection defence, and this study tests how far it goes using text and image versions of 191 question-answering sources across six multimodal models. Rendering does reduce simple attacks such as appending a marker or changing answer format for every model, but Qwen3-VL-8B still carried out image-borne answer replacement 77.96% of the time, and a search over just eight fixed attack variants largely closed the image gap for Qwen and Gemini, though not for GPT-5-nano. The takeaway is that pixels offer conditional protection that depends on the model, the attack goal and even wording and repetition, not a defence you can rely on.

### [Learning to Coordinate: LLM-Guided Workflow Evolution for Token-Efficient Red Teaming](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24221)

Multi-agent red-teaming systems combine complementary skills but waste tokens on repeated planning and redundant exchanges, and a target's response says whether an attack worked without saying which coordination choice should change. L2C represents each case's workflow as a versioned graph of agent roles, dependencies and input bindings, records what information actually flowed, and trains a controller to decide whether to keep the workflow, refine instructions, restructure collaboration or lock it, connecting feedback to explicit changes in the next run. It is evaluated on HarmBench with the aim of finding safety failures at lower token cost.
