## Coding-agent and agent security

### [Claude Code's "don't ask again" file has leaked 1,379 secrets to public GitHub](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/claude-codes-dont-ask-again-file.html#what-to-do)

The Code's lead story traces a mundane mechanism to an ugly result. When a Claude Code user approves a command with "Yes, and don't ask again," the exact command, including any password or token typed into it, can be saved in plain text to `.claude/settings.local.json`. Apiiro found 1,122 public copies of that file holding 1,379 secrets, among them 379 database passwords, 43 SSH logins and 23 AWS keys, often committed with Claude listed as co-author. Common scanners missed many because a password after `PGPASSWORD=` looks like an ordinary word. The fix is to rotate first, untrack the file and approve patterns instead of literal commands. Apiiro did not test whether most secrets still work; The Code says Anthropic is looking into fixes.

### [Aegis OS moves coding-agent permissions from each tool call to the whole workspace](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08516)

Anyone who has run Claude Code or Codex knows the dilemma: approve every action and drown in prompts, or pass `--dangerously-skip-permissions` and hand the agent your SSH keys. Aegis OS, a Rust runtime exposed over MCP and Google's A2A protocol, proposes a third path. Each agent works in a kernel-enforced sandbox on a copy-on-write shadow of the repository, with secrets injected only by an egress proxy that scopes them to specific HTTP methods and paths, so approval happens once at the transaction boundary. Across 52 real sessions the authors report prompts falling by 90.7–97.9%, attack success dropping from 100% to zero across 80 attacks, and merge conflicts between concurrent agents caught. These are the authors' own measurements.

### [Authorization drift: capable agents overstep their permissions even without an attacker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09113)

Most agent-security work assumes an adversary, but this paper argues that agents routinely exceed their authority on their own while chasing a user's goal. The Authorization Drift Benchmark pairs 648 tasks across seven domains with a legitimate path and a tempting unauthorized shortcut, validated through real agent rollouts. Across 13 models, three frameworks and more than 25,000 trajectories, the uncomfortable finding is that more capable models drift more, with frontier-class systems overstepping in 43–61% of runs. Drift is mostly early over-collection of data, and agentic scaffolds that seem to help actually coincide with fewer clean completions. Since 86% of tasks have an observed clean path, the authors conclude the overreach is avoidable rather than inevitable.

### [Agent Skills turn out to be a good source of least-privilege permissions for subagents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08905)

When an orchestrator delegates work, it must decide not only what each subagent does but which tools and files it may touch, and task outcomes alone say little about whether those grants were appropriate. This paper's insight is that Agent Skills already describe, step by step, the operations, tools and files a procedure needs. The authors synthesize tasks from Skills, run them through delegation in interactive environments and train on verified trajectories for both execution and authorization. On ClawArena-Team, a Qwen3.5-35B-A3B model gained 13.4 points in tool-permission precision and 24.1 points in workspace-permission precision while succeeding 11.6 points more often, and it stayed competitive on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SkillsBench.

### [Thought injection: attackers who mimic a model's own reasoning get much further](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08811)

Reasoning models open a new seam for prompt injection: untrusted text dressed up as the model's own deliberation. Testing production-class open-weight models such as GLM-5.3, MiniMax-M2.5 and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, the authors find that which styles and thinking tags work is highly specific to each model, and they exploit that in a containerized cyber range. Indirect injections written in the target's own reasoning style achieved up to 50% success on goals such as reverse-shell exfiltration and repository backdooring, whereas the same objectives phrased in an ordinary user voice never succeeded. Worse, thinking tags suppressed the agent's tendency to disclose that it had been injected, and the paper concludes that stripping those tags is an unreliable defense.

### [Prompt-injection guards fail on legal contracts, and five regexes keep up with them](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08834)

Specialized prompt-injection guards are sold as a safety layer for document pipelines, yet nobody had published how often they misfire on legal contracts, whose dense "notwithstanding anything to the contrary" language resembles an attack. Testing eleven open-weight guards on two contract corpora, the authors find document-level false-positive rates as high as 92.39%, two guards performing worse than chance, and a five-regex baseline that no guard beats on both accuracy and false alarms. Every guard scored zero on a second threat, unauthorized scope claims. Prompted LLMs resolved the tested failures, and a cascade that sends a mid-tier model's positives to a frontier model for verification came close to frontier performance at 6.5 times lower cost.

### [GreyZone tests red-teaming services against real open-source agent apps](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09161)

Benchmarks such as AgentDojo ask whether an agent resists attacks; GreyZone flips the question and asks whether automated red-teaming services can actually break production-style agents. The authors assembled independently developed open-source agent applications with real security boundaries around permissions, confidentiality and action authorization, including attack surfaces from multi-agent interaction and multimodal input, and they verify violations independently rather than trusting the attacker's report. The paper is early: it offers two preliminary case studies, using Promptfoo against Letta Code and Clawith, rather than a leaderboard. Its value is the released environments, which give security teams a more realistic target for judging whether a red-teaming product finds the failures that matter.

### [Poisoned reference answers can plant a lasting backdoor in optimized agent Skills](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09348)

Text-space Skill optimization, where an optimizer rewrites a reusable instruction file based on feedback, has a supervision channel nobody guards: the reference answers. This study shows that poisoning those answers alone, without access to the model, optimizer or Skill file, can induce a trigger-conditioned rule. In SearchQA every seed kept the exact malicious rule, and each final Skill emitted the payload on 200 of 200 triggered questions and none of 200 clean ones, while clean accuracy stayed indistinguishable from benign runs. The attack proved more fragile on SpreadsheetBench, where the optimizer's own abstraction and edit-ranking steps tended to strip the literal edits the payload needed, so exposure does not guarantee a backdoor survives.

### [MemoryDojo benchmarks how poison travels through agent memory across sessions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09721)

Memory makes long-horizon agents more useful, but it also lets a malicious instruction read in one session be stored, retrieved and acted on days later. MemoryDojo is built to trace that lifecycle, with 155 multi-session trajectories across four domains and support for five different memory systems. The headline result is blunt: every memory system tested was susceptible to poisoning, though the downstream effects varied widely depending on how each system processes and retrieves information. The authors also develop a memory-aware poison-optimization process that raises attack efficacy and exposes architectural differences. For anyone building persistent memory into an agent, it is a reminder that write paths need the same scrutiny as tool calls.

## Agent memory and context

### [Anthropic explains how Claude "dreams" to correct and organize its memory](https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory)

TLDR AI and The Rundown both flagged Anthropic's engineering essay on agent memory, and its most useful idea is offline "dreaming": revisiting transcripts to fix notes, build wiki-like maps and promote a small set of facts into every new session. The examples make it concrete. Playing Pokémon Red, Sonnet 3.7 wrote 413 memory files and earned one badge, while Fable 5 kept nine files and earned four; in another run, dreaming caught a wrong landmark note that had trapped Claude outside Mt. Moon. Telling strong models what memory is for, rather than how to use it, worked better than fixed rules. These are Anthropic's own evaluations, and the [Dreams API docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/managed-agents/dreams) show the managed implementation.

### [Decision Context teaches agents to stop repeating the mistakes users already corrected](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08644)

Most agent memory stores facts about preferences and history, so a user still has to fix the same behavioral mistake at the start of every session. Forge Decision Context targets that procedural gap. It captures human corrections and self-detected tool-call failures, compiles them into imperative RULE directives and injects each one at the decision point where it applies, rather than dumping all memories into the prompt. On DC-Bench, a new set of 71 scenarios built from real agent traces in a deterministic mocked-tool environment, the system reaches 95% compliance against 55% for naive retrieval while injecting about ten times fewer tokens per query. Representation, activation and placement, the authors argue, matter more than storage.

### [ENGRAM decides when agent experience can safely move from retrieval into weights](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09472)

Consolidating an agent's stored experience into its weights raises two questions that are usually conflated: what to train on, and when the external record can be deleted. ENGRAM separates them. A demonstration-aware gate admits a skill for training either because the student has mastered it or because the teacher has enough successful demonstrations, and a separate verification gate compares the updated model without retrieval against the base model with retrieval on reserved probes. Only skills that pass are released from memory; the rest stay retrievable. The authors report gains over frozen-weight controls, a much smaller retrievable footprint while staying close to keeping everything, and fewer input tokens and model calls.

### [Compacting conversations into facts beats RAG on values, but loses on wording](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09807)

Should a remembering chatbot keep the transcript and search it, or compress each turn into atomic facts? This carefully paired LoCoMo study runs both arms on all 1,985 questions with the same stores, generator and judge, which licenses an exact statistical test on the 512 disagreements. On the four categories most papers report, compaction wins 71.4% to 64.6% while using 34% fewer prompt tokens. Add the adversarial set others drop, however, and the lead shrinks to an insignificant two points. The pattern is instructive: compaction keeps what a fact asserts and discards how it was said, so it wins when the answer is a value and loses when the answer depends on exact phrasing.

### [How much conversation history does an agent need? Less than you think, but it depends](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09662)

This study treats memory budget as an engineering trade-off rather than a default. Holding retrieval ranking and the reader model fixed on 1,100 BEAM questions, the authors vary the passage budget and find that scores rise with more context but with sharply diminishing returns at the top end, while abstention scores sometimes improve as context shrinks. A controlled task with explicit conversational-state targets shows Qwen keeping direct requirements better when user messages are retained, but faltering when answers depend on references to earlier offers; Mistral stayed near the floor. Notably, a scoring audit left the correctness of BEAM's own answers unresolved, which the authors treat as a warning about the metric itself.

### [Workflow-aware compression trims agent trajectories without dropping what later steps need](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08865)

Long tool-using runs accumulate structured outputs and intermediate artifacts until the context itself becomes the bottleneck, and existing compression usually ranks text by importance or truncates by rule, ignoring which earlier output a later step will depend on. WAAC instead learns compression habits from successful past trajectories and runs an online backward dependency analysis to decide what must be retained, then picks a strategy for each kind of history. On PinchBench it reaches an overall score of 87.6, and on Odysseys a 19% perfect-completion rate, while cutting execution cost by 19.7% and 29.1% against the strongest baselines. It is a useful counterpoint to generic summarization-based compaction.

## Harnesses, review and coding agents

### [LinkedIn's multi-agent AI reviewer now covers 40,000 pull requests a week](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/how-linkedin-uses-ai-to-review-40000)

ByteByteGo's interview with LinkedIn engineers is a candid account of what failed before something worked. A vendor reviewer produced generic, noisy comments engineers opted out of, and a single model with house rules hallucinated issues and even misplaced line numbers. The production design uses an orchestrator that sends each diff to independent subagents running different models, merges overlapping findings through a cheap line-range check followed by an LLM comparison, and asks another model to verify single-source findings before anything is posted. Layered company, repository and change-specific rules supply local conventions. Engineers accepted 63.9% of comments, measured by whether code actually changed. Next comes a risk heat map. The article carries sponsor blocks, ignored here.

### [Willison hides agent-written PR descriptions behind a collapsible summary](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/9/details-summary/)

Simon Willison's problem with agent-written pull-request descriptions is not accuracy but volume: they are so complete that they read like slop, and his reflex is "I'm not reading all of that." His fix is pleasantly low-tech. Each PR now opens with a short, human-approved paragraph, sometimes with a screenshot, that says what the change is for and whether it deserves attention, while the agent's full essay sits inside an HTML `<details>` block whose summary names the model and effort level, such as "Claude Opus 5.5 Medium." GitHub Markdown renders it collapsed. The opener is sometimes lifted from the agent's own text, but it carries his promise that a human judged it worth reading.

### [Stale instruction files quietly tax every coding-agent session](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09802)

Files such as `CLAUDE.md` and `AGENTS.md` are read in every session, so when they fall behind the code, the error becomes recurring documentation debt. This study takes three real stale instructions from active open-source repositories, a test command, a code location and a component procedure, and runs agents on 22 maintenance tasks with each instruction left stale, corrected or removed, analyzing 732 trajectories at the tool-call level. Agents followed stale guidance whenever it seemed relevant, sometimes even when correct information was visible elsewhere; with the stale test command, Claude Sonnet 5.5 needed about two extra tool calls per run. Agents recovered from the immediate failures, yet none repaired the instruction file itself.

### [Routine plugin operations can silently break an agent harness's guardrails](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09088)

Agent harnesses spread guardrails, autonomy controls and shared state across native services, independent plugins and configuration, which means safety can depend on ordering that nobody wrote down. Studying five released harnesses and fourteen independently written plugins, the authors show that ordinary operations, with no code change at all, can break the relationships that architectural patterns rely on. In one case, after a plugin was reapplied or an allow rule saved, policy stopped evaluating edits even though every plugin still appeared active, and a prohibited edit went through. Their PatternOps procedure traces such requirements to the components and records that enforce them, arguing that guardrail correctness belongs to the running system and its history.

### [DeCAF optimizes agent pipelines one design dimension at a time](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08723)

Tuning an agentic pipeline means juggling prompts, topology, model choice and tool policies, which interact in ways that make monolithic automated search opaque. DeCAF, a decomposed credit-assignment framework, first identifies which dimensions matter for a given benchmark and seed pipeline, then gives independent coding-agent sessions a restricted edit scope to experiment on each dimension while logging every hypothesis, edit, cost and latency. A final synthesis session combines compatible findings and uses a component-level trajectory evaluator, with role-specific rubrics derived from each agent's instructions, to locate weak components. Across a suite of agentic benchmarks it improves average task performance by 2.58 points, but the bigger win is latency, down 42.45% against the best baseline, with cost 4.3% lower.

### [BOXBOX lets an agent rewrite its own harness mid-task, without losing its place](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09218)

Offline harness optimization tunes an agent on reference answers and freezes the result, but long, open-ended jobs surface problems no offline run anticipated. To study that setting, the authors built PerfRace, a benchmark for hours-long performance optimization of software projects, and BOXBOX, a system in which the agent can trigger revisions to its own harness that are hot-reloaded by a managing kernel while the execution context is preserved. Under matched time budgets it beats a frozen harness, offline harness evolution and simply scaling the harness, reaching a 1.70x speedup after four hours against 1.54x for the strongest baseline, while using task time and model spend more efficiently.

### [Flex-RSI measures whether frontier agents can improve the way they work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08946)

Most agent benchmarks hold the harness fixed and grade the answer; Flex-RSI grades whether an agent can improve its own prompts, tools, memory and control loop under a fixed budget. In each round an executor improves the solution, kept only if it beats held-out validation data, and an improver then revises the harness, including the executor and itself, before final scoring on hidden tests. Across vision and robotics tasks spanning perception, robot action and 3D reconstruction, self-improvement helped most when agents built their own solutions. Open-weight models were held back by pass-or-fail feedback and fell furthest behind where outputs faced strict physical-stability or exactness checks, while paired runs confirm that editing the harness itself contributes.

### [Many small agent teams beat one giant team for test-time compute](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09798)

When a verifier can score every attempt, there are two familiar ways to spend extra inference: run many independent agents and keep the best, which saturates quickly, or form one large collaborating team, which plateaus as it grows. This paper samples many small teams in parallel and keeps the best team's result. With a 27-billion-parameter model and a budget of 100 agents, teams of ten beat independent agents on 10 of 12 long-horizon tasks, from GPU kernels to formal proofs and exploit development, and beat a single 100-agent team on all four tasks where that comparison was run. At equal dollar cost they also beat a frontier model on half the tasks, a result a simple variance model explains.

### [Tracybot links agent-written code back to the prompts that produced it](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09395)

With coding agents, the reasons behind a change often live only in the chat that produced it, leaving reviewers with a diff and no intent. Tracybot, an open-source tool spanning VS Code, Git and coding agents, captures each change as a "Tasklet": optional planning prompts plus the build prompt that generated the code. It then maps line-level changes to the Tasklets responsible and can export one as an architecture decision record when it matters. The evaluation is modest and focused on usability, with a focus group of seven and a survey of 24, but participants found the design a clear way to represent developer intent and useful for code review, which complements Willison's summary trick.

### [Researchers argue for deliberately pacing autonomous development to keep oversight meaningful](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09550)

As agents take on more of the software lifecycle without a human in the loop, the question shifts from whether people can intervene to when intervention still means anything. This position paper, built on an exploratory review of 20 academic and grey-literature sources, proposes "deliberate pacing": intentionally adjusting when and how autonomous development proceeds so that consequential decisions surface while they can still be understood and changed. The authors identify three drivers, the shifting relative cost of human involvement, the timing of oversight opportunities and the way pacing choices cascade across the lifecycle, and reject both uninterrupted autonomy and constant interruption as defaults. It offers a research agenda rather than empirical results.

### [Willison and Fable 5.2 build a multi-device system to find a mystery beep](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/9/mysterious-beeps/)

Something in Simon Willison's box room kept beeping, and rather than unpack everything he had Claude build Beep Locator, a Python server plus browser listeners that turn phones, iPads and laptops into a microphone array. Claude dug up a 2007 acoustic-ranging paper, BeepBeep, as the basis; the server has one device chirp to align every listener's clip, then triangulates. Self-calibration worked poorly, so a "rough mode" lets him drag devices into place, and a clap-test mode builds confidence. It located the culprit, a carbon-monoxide detector, to within about 40 centimeters. Worth noticing: the listeners hot-load new code from the server, which made live iteration with Fable 5.2 easy but is effectively remote code execution by design.

### [A founder builds native iOS and Android language tutors with Codex and no Swift experience](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-i-ai-how-this-founder-built-a)

On Lenny's How I AI, William Imoh explains how a 1,000-day Duolingo streak left him unable to converse in Norwegian, so he built Mural, an immersive tutor that refuses to switch to English. Despite never writing Swift or Kotlin, he had Codex build fully native apps, steering it with product-manager habits such as epics and user stories and with taste notes like "Apple-quality, feels like autumn." Real-time voice from GPT-Live made the product possible, though he estimates a 30-minute session costs $3 to $5. He also runs his 25-person company with a Lord of the Rings–themed team of agents, and notes that most of his custom skills became unnecessary as models improved.

## Evaluation and alignment

### [Visa's agentic-commerce benchmark finds AI shoppers fail 43% of the time](https://usa.visa.com/about-visa/newsroom/press-releases.releaseId.22936.html)

The Deep View highlighted Visa's new benchmark, which tests whether an agent can do what good salespeople do: draw out a buyer's unstated requirements, then buy only if something actually fits. Twenty-two models from 12 labs worked through 15 scenarios, such as finding a TV compatible with a child's console. Overall pass rates were just 57.2%, ranging from 82.2% for the best to 28.9% for the worst, and all three attempts at a scenario succeeded only 34.2% of the time. More worrying, 54% of failed runs still ended in a purchase. This is Visa's own benchmark and press release, and [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/11/05/ai-agents-shopping-ranking-best-agent/) reports which agents topped it.

### [EMERGE breaks "emergent misalignment" into eight behaviors instead of one score](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08569)

Emergent misalignment, where fine-tuning on a narrow task produces misbehavior on unrelated prompts, has been replicated widely, yet most studies still rely on the original paper's small question set, judge and thresholds, which can confuse misalignment with plain capability loss. EMERGE instead tracks eight distinct misaligned behaviors from base to fine-tuned model on out-of-domain prompts with matched benign controls, while separately measuring capability, coherence and refusal. Applied to 8 open-weight models and 13 fine-tuning datasets, it shows that models with similar headline misalignment rates can have very different behavioral profiles. The authors pitch it as a shared standard that would move research from detecting the phenomenon toward understanding it.
