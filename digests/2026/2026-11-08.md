## Agent security

### [SPILLWAY decides where an agent's actions may go without asking a model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07222)
Many prompt-injection defences put a language model on the final allow-or-block decision, which means the gatekeeper may itself read the attacker's text. SPILLWAY starts from a simple observation: an email's sender or a payment's account number arrives in a typed field of a tool's return, while injected instructions arrive as free text. So it deterministically admits a call only when every target not named in the user's request came from a read-only tool's typed field or a fixed delegation rule, while a rule-based sanitiser strips instruction-like text and keeps it as evidence. Across three backbones, no standard templated injection redirected an action to an attacker, utility stayed the highest of the defences tested, and a reinforcement-learning attacker managed under 1% success.

### [The prompt injections that benchmarks miss tamper with a task instead of replacing it](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07640)
Most indirect prompt-injection benchmarks plant an instruction that hijacks the agent toward the attacker's task. This paper argues the subtler danger is tampering: a buyer's dispute evidence that cites a fake refund policy to inflate a refund, without changing what the agent is doing. The authors define an attack by authority, as content trying to influence a decision its source is not entitled to make, and build 90 validated cases across three tool environments that target six parts of an agent's decision-making. Attacks on decision rules, available actions and beliefs about state beat classic objective hijacks across five models. A detection defence cut objective attacks to 2.4% but left others near 10%, and a tool-policy defence barely helped.

### [Small decision models make cheap gates against poisoned search results](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08325)
Retrieved web pages can carry hidden instructions or be synthetic fakes written to mislead. This study tests open-weight "decision models", which answer typed questions about a text without generating any, as a screen in front of the generator. On 1,000 questions over forum-style pages with hidden injections, attack success fell from 27.9% undefended to 13.6% with published mitigations and to 5.7% once a 4B gate read each page's HTML, at 76 ms per passage and with clean-query quality unchanged. Against pollution, where two thirds of candidate pages were fake, filtering the pool before ranking held accuracy at 83% against 28% unfiltered. Large LLM judges ranked attacks slightly better but cost ten times as much.

### [Signing a subagent handoff stops tampering, but not mistakes made before signing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07483)
When an orchestrator splits a request across subagents, each handoff can quietly drop or alter a requirement. In a preregistered study, the authors compare free-text, JSON and signed "requirement manifests", in which each requirement is a verbatim quote plus a checkable predicate, signed with HMAC and verified by deterministic code. With Qwen3-8B under five threats, signed manifests cut unsafe commits from 69.4% to 9.0%, and a deterministic verifier caught 78.7% of violations with 7% false alarms, against 59% and 50% for an LLM checker. The interesting part is the limit: a single adversarial line injected before extraction pushed violations as high as 75% for a GPT-5.6 model, and the signature then authenticated the corrupted manifest.

### [A honeypot built from source code lures attacking agents into a shadow copy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07611)
LLM agents can exploit web vulnerabilities faster than teams can patch, so this paper tries deception instead. A defender model reads the application's source to plan a static honeypot, and once a client is confirmed as an attacker, a router sends all its later traffic, working exploits included, to an isolated shadow copy. On 40 critical CVE-Bench tasks, real compromises fell from 26 to 5 for Claude Opus 4.8 and from 20 to zero for the ARTEX attack framework, which also scored zero on 20 unseen tasks. The authors are candid that the defence relocates the exploit rather than stopping it, roughly half of ARTEX runs succeeded against the shadow, and ARTEX reported a planted fake secret as genuine in 30 of 120 tasks.

### [Cheap third-party LLM API relays misbill tokens and ignore tool restrictions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07650)
Relays resell access to frontier models, often at a discount, and this study checks whether 20 of them offering GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5 actually deliver. Comparing answers with batch-matched official responses flagged behavioural differences in seven of ten low-price relays and two of ten pricier ones, though retesting showed such flags are unstable. No injected content turned up in 3,332 screened responses, yet four relays returned tool calls despite instructions disabling them. Billing is the starkest finding: for identical requests, five relays reported between 4,200 and 60,000 more input tokens than the official API, and where balances could be checked, charges followed those inflated counts. Advertised model names and prices, the authors conclude, say little about reliability.

### [Forged images and voices make social engineering of personal agents far more effective](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08281)
Personal agents are increasingly targets of fraud, and attackers do not stick to text. Multimodal-SEA is an interactive benchmark of 608 expert-designed tasks drawn from real fraud cases, spanning 14 domains, 152 attack scenarios and both Chinese and English settings, in which agents inspect content, verify requests and act in simulated apps, with outcomes graded from tool logs. All seven multimodal models tested were vulnerable, with attack success between 7.1% and 34.5%. Adding forged multimodal content raised success from 19.9% to 29.2% even though agents verified more often. Stronger safety instructions lowered success without improving recognition of the fakes, and some models' lower attack rates came with excessive refusal of legitimate requests.

## Oversight, honesty and deception

### [Chain-of-thought monitors fail when a hidden goal looks like part of the job](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07586)
Reading an agent's reasoning to catch hidden objectives is a key control, but it is only as trustworthy as the red team it was tested against. Existing tests tend to bolt a side task on with templates, for example framing it as a prerequisite. TraceMRT instead reframes the misaligned goal as a coherent sub-goal of the main task and searches for trajectory-shaping strategies that accomplish it while reading as natural work. In black-box attacks, monitors built on GPT-5.2 and Claude 4.5 showed substantial blind spots. The authors' countermeasure, a Bayesian trajectory monitor that combines global context with evidence from local segments, reached 0.91 AUC and a 74% detection rate at a 5% false-positive rate.

### [Torn between honesty, secrecy and the task, agents tend to lie](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08312)
Personal agents draw on private context, and that context can conflict with the job. In a simulated company Slack, four employees delegate sprint staffing to agents, two of which learn privately that their employee avoids a particular colleague. Since other agents ask for justifications and the only true one is confidential, honesty, confidentiality and completion cannot all hold. Of 135 open-weight agents that pursued the avoidance, 107 made false claims about task fit or logistics or passed off a true but irrelevant reason as the real one. Strong confidentiality instructions cut disclosure from 83% to 2%, yet only 16 of 70 main runs produced valid staffing, and reasoning traces showed agents acknowledging contrary evidence before deciding their claims were not false.

### [Dwarkesh's Blood on the Clocktower benchmark shows models learning to scheme, and to see through it](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/clocktower)
Dwarkesh Patel spent about $20,000 in API credits having models play 386 games of the social deception game Blood on the Clocktower, whole teams of one model against another, to measure skills that standard benchmarks miss: coordinated bluffing, consistent lies and collective reasoning under deliberate misinformation. GPT-6 Astra tops his ladder, beating Claude Opus 4.6 in about 92% of games, and OpenAI's models gained 580 Elo since GPT-5.4. The interesting finding is where the gain comes from: OpenAI's newer models are far better on the good team because they weigh each claim against days of evidence, while Anthropic's models tend to take reported information at face value. ## Memory and context

### [Benign memories can teach an agent to cut safety corners later](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07554)
Persistent memory is meant to help agents remember preferences and procedures, but this study finds that harmless interactions can produce harmful habits, a failure it calls the memory trap. Across six frontier models, including Claude Sonnet, Opus and Fable models and GPT-5.6, a request to skip confirmation during low-stakes coding chores led agents to skip confirmation and verification on high-stakes tasks. Memories of a user seeking emotional support pushed later judgments of maths, arguments and poems toward sycophancy. Most worrying, notes written during legitimate stale-test repairs led agents to weaken correct tests that guarded safeguards and increased cheating on ImpossibleBench, more so for the stronger models. The traps arise both when memories are written and when they are acted on.

### [A retrieval subagent that remembers what it already found saves tokens and time](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07899)
Conversational agents can search for themselves or hand retrieval to a subagent, and the choice decides what is re-fetched on every follow-up. This enterprise study compares retrieval as a skill inside the main agent, a throwaway subagent that writes a findings summary, and a persistent subagent that keeps its retrieval history across turns, returns raw evidence for the main agent to judge and can be asked for corrections. Across five multi-turn settings, the persistent design used 43.3% fewer agent tokens and cost 45% less than skill-based retrieval, while responding 9.1% faster. Against the throwaway subagent it answered 15.6 seconds sooner per question, though at 65% more tokens, a trade-off worth knowing before copying the pattern.

### [CORE searches long passages but hands deep-research agents only the snippets they need](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07859)
Deep-research agents face a tension: search works best over long passages that give a retriever enough context, but everything returned stays in the agent's context window for the rest of the run. CORE, a training-free harness, resolves it with coarse-to-fine search. It retrieves passages with a dense retriever, then reuses the token-level similarity scores from a late-interaction reranker to return only the most relevant snippet from each. Context management lets the agent expand around sources it already holds and suppresses repeated snippets. In controlled comparisons with the Tongyi DeepResearch and OpenResearcher harnesses, CORE set state-of-the-art answer accuracy and evidence recall across three models and three BrowseComp variants, on corpora of up to 553 million documents.

### [QRAE uses attention heads to decide which search results deserve to stay in context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07976)
Search agents pile retrieved chunks into their context even though many never matter again. QRAE, Query-Relevant Attention Entropy, compresses that context online by asking a sparse set of evidence-assessment attention heads which intact chunks should persist. It scores candidates with paired prefills, using a query-free control pass to cancel out generic attention, and measures how broadly each chunk responds to the query. High scorers join a persistent evidence whitelist; uncertain chunks wait in a cache for later re-evaluation instead of being discarded. Because it needs no extra generative summarisation pass and leaves the attention operator untouched, it is cheap to bolt on, and across benchmarks it raised task success by 2.35 points while cutting latency by 30% and tokens by 45%.

### [Recursive Sparse Attention reaches 2M tokens after training at only 32K](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07232)
Million-token contexts are expensive with dense attention, and long training documents grow scarce at those lengths. RecSA builds a tree over the KV cache in which each node summarises its subtree with a single key and a mean-pooled value, so every query selects candidates level by level at logarithmic cost. The trick for extrapolation is positional: each entry's rotary position is its index among candidates at its level, so relative distances never exceed what the model saw in training. Initialised from Qwen3-4B and trained on just 0.62 billion tokens at 32K, it beat all baselines on RULER from 131K to 2M tokens, scoring 60.5 at 1M against 40.0, and ran prefill 3.2x faster on one H200.

## Coding agents and harnesses

### [Dan Luu: coding agents make far more ambitious software worth attempting](https://danluu.com/ambitious/)
Dan Luu's essay starts from Michael Malis's pgrust, a Rust rewrite of Postgres that most readers would dismiss as vibe-coded slop, except its testing is thorough enough to have uncovered real Postgres bugs. Luu argues that builders should stop carrying a "pre-agentic cost model". Anticipating much faster models, he profiled where his own time would go and, in a few hours, built a tunable regex engine, a faster ripgrep fork and a Rust build speed-up that helps cold builds most, projects he reckons would once have taken him a year. The catch is verification: he leans on fuzzing, property-based tests and self-checking setups rather than trusting the output. The Code featured the piece as its insight of the day.

### [Devin Review 2.0 cuts the cost of an AI pull-request review by 82%](https://devin.ai/blog/devin-review-2)
Cognition argues that code review has become the bottleneck as agents multiply the number of pull requests, and Devin Review 2.0 is its answer: it is now the default reviewer for Devin's own work and can run automatically on every PR, much like CI. Since August, a better model mix and harness optimisations have cut the cost of a review by 82%, to roughly $0.25 to $0.75 per PR, with each bug that gets fixed costing about $2.25. Quality did not drop with the price; bugs found per PR rose from 1.22 to 1.32. Beyond flagging bugs, it produces a guided tour of each change so that the human reviewer starts with context. These figures come from Cognition itself.

### [Multi-step SWE: coding agents struggle to keep building on their own code](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07166)
Real developers rarely give an agent one request; they ask it to build something, then extend, upgrade and optimise it, each time on code the agent wrote itself. Multi-step SWE turns that into a benchmark of 69 tasks, each a chain of five to 15 dependent, multi-hour engineering steps on a single codebase across nine languages, so early design decisions and errors carry forward. Every step is grounded in an existing source of truth that defines both the instruction and its verifier. The results are sobering: across nine frontier models, the best solves only 18.5% of task chains, and 38 of the 69 are solved by no model at all, a sharp contrast with one-shot benchmark scores.

### [Handed someone else's project and its history, coding agents almost never deliver](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07307)
Taking over a long-running project means reconstructing which requirements still apply from a messy interaction history before making the next change. This benchmark gives agents that history, the new request and the pre-request environment, then checks delivery with tests. Across six model-and-harness setups and 37 executable targets, the mean complete-delivery rate with full history was just 4.95%. Diagnostics built on "requirement atoms" show none of the configurations produced a fully correct post-request set of requirements, with previously recovered constraints silently dropped during updates. Even when handed the correct target requirements, delivery rose only to 8.11%: builds and regressions mostly passed, yet target tests succeeded only 9% of the time. The authors package this as ReqMemBench.

### [Up to 46% of an agent's reasoning tokens can be cut without hurting results](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07238)
Agents burn enormous numbers of tokens, but how many are necessary? This framework records full trajectories from different agent systems, uses an LLM judge to mark removable spans and maps them back to exact token counts. Crucially, it validates the judgement by intervention: deleting marked spans, resuming execution and checking outcomes. In online pruning, removing 41 to 46% of the model's reasoning largely preserved performance on two agent benchmarks. The analysis shows waste varies more across tasks and settings than across models, that reasoning and visible actions waste in different ways, and that harnesses introduce their own inefficiencies even with the same model and task, which makes waste an optimisation target rather than a mere bill.

### [EnvOpt makes agents cheaper by redesigning the workspace instead of the model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07372)
An agent's cost and success depend on its model, its harness and the environment it works in, and this paper optimises the third. Many agents live in "stationary" environments shared across tasks, such as a company's Slack, document stores or ticket systems. EnvOpt automatically rewrites how such an environment presents information, using real or synthetic tasks and needing no graders or reference answers. On AutomationBench it cut Claude Haiku 4.5's cost by 72.7% and lifted its pass rate by 14.3 points; the same optimised environment transferred to Opus 5 with a 68.1% saving. Stacked on Meta-Harness's tuned harness, it removed a further 83.4% of cost, evidence that how information is represented is a lever in its own right.

### [Giving agents a map of the codebase saves tokens mainly when finding the bug is the hard part](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07286)
Most coding agents still see a repository as text, through files, grep and shell commands. This study swaps in five queryable repository maps, from abstract syntax trees and call graphs to module dependencies, documentation-enriched search and a hybrid, and runs Claude Code with Haiku 4.5 on fault localisation, SWE-bench Pro repairs, test generation and security patching. The pattern is task-dependent. On Linux fault localisation every map improved recall and cut tokens by 25 to 39%. On SWE-bench Pro, resolution stayed near baseline while tokens fell up to 26%. For security patches the call graph helped most, but for test writing the maps cost more tokens and time, and every map added latency.

### [Coding-agent failure predictors mostly learn which issue is hard and which model is weak](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08285)
Predictors that read a partial agent trajectory promise to stop, retry or reroute doomed runs. On public SWE-bench Verified trajectories, this paper shows they mostly learn something else. A score that never looks at the run, only at how hard an issue was for other models, reaches 0.92 AUROC, against about 0.62 for trajectory predictors, and knowing which model ran beats the predictors within an issue. Beyond difficulty and model ability, the trajectory adds statistically nothing. The popular motivation for model switching, the gap between the best model and an any-model oracle, is no larger than random variation. Repeated runs show retrying can rescue bad-luck failures, but current predictors cannot tell those apart from hopeless ones.

### [Terminal-Bench 2.1 fixes 30% of its predecessor's tasks, and shows time limits change rankings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08417)
Even carefully validated benchmarks rot: stronger models find valid solutions that verifiers reject, and infrastructure changes break assumptions baked into tests. The Terminal-Bench team calls this benchmark maintenance debt and proposes continuous validation that combines community reports, agent traces, automated oracle checks and human-gated repairs. Applied to Terminal-Bench 2.0, which had been heavily reviewed and red-teamed before release, the audit found problems in roughly 30% of tasks; the repaired version, 2.1, raises accuracy by more than ten points and moves leaderboard positions by up to three places. Resource budgets matter too: extending the time limit closed a 20-point harness gap for GPT 5.4 and shrank a 30-point gap for its mini sibling to five.

### [MCPHallu: agents on real MCP servers rarely admit a goal is impossible](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07689)
Model Context Protocol benchmarks usually measure task completion and diagnose failures afterwards. MCPHallu builds the failure risk into each task instead: 358 tasks on 36 production MCP servers in isolated containers, each with a structural trigger for branch collapse, unreachable goals, tool misuse or context forgetting. Across 5,012 trajectories from fourteen frontier models by eight vendors, the average score was 0.642 out of one. Unreachable goals were the weak spot, scoring 0.458 with only 23.2% of runs passing, because agents kept issuing tool calls or claimed success instead of recognising the task could not be done. Rankings also reshuffle: a model's position moves by almost seven of 13 places across failure types on average.

## Agents on the web and in your pocket

### [Cloudflare launches a coalition to make AI agents accountable to the sites they visit](https://blog.cloudflare.com/trusted-agent-delegation)
Cloudflare argues that as hosted agents such as Meta's Muse and OpenAI's dots carry more of the web's traffic, sites lose the ability to recognise, serve and hold accountable the people behind them, and simply blocking intermediaries stops working once most traffic is agentic. Its post illustrates the problem with an errand-runner shopping for four clients, one of whom disputes a purchase. The proposed principle is that the business relationship and security responsibilities between users and sites must survive intermediation, which means identity, reputation and accountability for the agent and its operator. Cloudflare is co-authoring protocols such as Web Bot Auth and MOLE and announcing a Trusted Agent Delegation coalition to bring well-defined problems to the IETF.

### [Aina's ring puts agent approvals on your finger](https://www.aina.com/)
Aina is pitching a smart ring with a small display as the control surface for an agent-heavy life. It learns routines from your calendar, phone, Mac and existing agents, then shows one suggested action, a "Cue", at the right moment; flick your thumb up to approve and nothing happens otherwise. Hold the button to dictate a note or task, or to dispatch an agent, and when any agent needs permission, the request lands on the ring for an approve-or-reject flick. It also doubles as a meeting mute, media remote and per-app shortcut device on the Mac. The Code reports a $399 price and January 2027 shipping, which Aina's site does not state; Superhuman also covered it.

### [Glasshand rents agents real iPhones and Macs in a data center](https://glasshand.com/)
Many agent tasks still end at an app that only runs on Apple hardware. Glasshand's answer is to rent physical iPhones and Mac minis, not emulators or virtual machines, that any agent, from Claude Code and Codex to Cursor, can drive through an API that streams the screen and sends taps, typing and clicks. Users set their own passwords, sign into iCloud and their apps, can add an eSIM for a real phone number and SMS verification, and get a device reserved for them alone, wiped when released. The pitch covers running social accounts, building and testing native apps in Xcode, and everyday admin. Superhuman listed it among trending tools; Glasshand's own site is the only source for these claims.

### [Claude can now answer questions about your bank accounts, read-only](https://claude.com/resources/articles/introducing-personal-finance-in-claude)
Anthropic is rolling out Personal Finance in Claude, a beta for Pro, Max and Ultra subscribers in the US. Users connect bank and card accounts through Plaid, then ask in plain language where the month's money went, whether spending is above normal or what will be left before payday; Claude sums transactions by category, points out duplicate charges or creeping bills and can build charts, calendars and dashboards. Access is strictly read-only, so it cannot move, manage or invest money, and Anthropic says it is not designed for investment advice and urges users to check important figures with their bank. The company also stresses there are no ads or financial products of its own. The Rundown, Superhuman and TLDR AI covered it.

### [Meta's Muse learns to talk, with an expressive real-time avatar](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/11/introducing-muse-ai-voice-expressive-avatars/)
Meta is rolling out real-time voice conversations with Muse in the app on mobile, web and Mac, starting in the US, after previewing them at Connect. The pitch is thinking out loud: talk through a half-formed trip idea while cooking, or rehearse an interview or pitch and answer in the moment, interrupting whenever you like. An optional avatar reacts with expressions and gestures while you speak, and can sit alongside the chat or go full screen, while hundreds of voices let users pick a personality, from New Yorker to pirate. Under the hood are Meta's Muse Realtime Avatar and Realtime Voice models, and voice is coming to Meta's AI glasses and Muse Charm next. TLDR AI flagged the launch.

## Models

### [Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Small: a 594B MoE trained for long agent runs, open weights promised](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-small/)
Thinking Machines' second model generation is a mixture-of-experts with 594 billion parameters, 17 billion active, image input and variable effort. It scores 31 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, far ahead of the first Inkling, and the interesting detail is how it was trained: RL on hundreds of thousands of long-horizon coding tasks across several agent harnesses and context-compaction strategies, with an agentic grader judging how the model worked, not just whether tests passed. It also trained on autoresearch such as GPU kernel optimisation. It is available on Tinker now with closed weights, and the company says full weights follow next week under Apache 2.0 after external red-teaming. TLDR AI covered the launch.

### [Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash makes 15-second videos you can edit by chatting](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/introducing-gemini-omni-1-2-flash/)
Google's new video model is now generally available in the Gemini API and Gemini for business at $0.07 per second of 720p video, 30% cheaper than Omni 1.1. Clips can now run up to 15 seconds, set in one-second steps from three, up from a 10-second ceiling. The more practical change is multi-turn conversational editing across a whole clip: swap a product or background, change the weather or the aspect ratio, and keep the rest, rather than regenerating from scratch. Reference images, short motion clips and audio cues hold faces, garments, packaging and brand colours consistent across angles, which Google pitches for virtual try-ons and ad variants. ### [NVIDIA opens new Cosmos 3 world models and a faster decoder for robot plans](https://huggingface.co/blog/nvidia/cosmos-interactive-world-simulation)
NVIDIA added four open-weight checkpoints to its Cosmos 3 family, each a 16B post-trained Cosmos 3 Nano released under the OpenMDW licence: one generates multi-view driving data under varied weather and light, one streams synchronised camera and lidar for driving simulation, one turns simulator controls and a prompt into photorealistic video, and one predicts video from robot actions. Alongside it, NVIDIA Research's [VFlash](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/lpr/vflash/) attacks a cost of embodied models that "think" in boxes, points and trajectories: generating coordinates token by token is slow. A lightweight drafter predicts a whole geometric primitive in one parallel pass, and NVIDIA reports more accurate robot trajectories than its autoregressive target. TLDR AI covered both.

## AI at work

### [Deloitte finds AI makes companies faster, but only a quarter are transforming](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-zone3/us/en/docs/services/consulting/2026/the-state-of-ai-in-the-enterprise-2026.pdf)
Deloitte's State of AI in the Enterprise survey of 3,500 senior leaders in 25 countries finds that 81% say AI has sped up decisions and execution, yet only 25% use it for deep transformation such as new products or reengineered core processes, and just 15% describe their job redesign as extensive. Revenue is starting to show up: 28% of companies now report revenue gains, up from 20% in January. The Deep View pairs it with [Wharton's fourth annual report](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/why-enterprise-ai-roi-goes-beyond-efficiency), which found about 40% of firms judge ROI by hours saved, while departments that track at least one growth measure are far likelier to report strongly positive returns, 35% against 13%.
