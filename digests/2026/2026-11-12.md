## Frontier models and workplace tools

### [OpenAI lets businesses claim a ChatGPT profile and, in the US, a shopper-facing agent](https://openai.com/index/introducing-businesses-chatgpt)

OpenAI opened a "Businesses in ChatGPT" beta on Tuesday, giving companies a verified profile inside a product it says reaches 1.2 billion people a week — a vendor figure. Through Business Hub, a verified firm can update its details, add a product feed, and see aggregated mentions, website clicks and "discovery topics." OpenAI says it does not share users' chats, and that insights appear only above a privacy threshold. The more interesting piece is Business Agents, live first for eligible US ecommerce firms: when ChatGPT names a business, a customer can open a clearly labeled, separate conversation with that firm's agent. Brands can also pay to make the agent the landing spot for ChatGPT Ads, with those placements labeled.

### [Anthropic puts Meetings, Sheets and Whiteboards inside Claude, and bets context is the lock-in](https://claude.com/resources/articles/sheets-whiteboards-data)

Anthropic spent Tuesday widening Claude into a workplace suite, and The Deep View's reading is that the real prize is the context already sitting in it. Claude Meetings, in beta for Team and Enterprise, joins a Google Meet call as a visible participant and — Anthropic says — leaves if an outside guest arrives unapproved. While people talk, it can draft a plan on a Whiteboard, answer questions and pull figures from connected apps, with the notes landing in Claude afterward. Whiteboards and Sheets, in beta on paid plans, let a team brainstorm or build a spreadsheet, then export to Excel. Claude Data is generally available, and interactive connectors can now open full apps from Adobe, Atlassian and HubSpot beside the chat.

### [Google's Gemini Decision 1 scores a thousand questions in one pass, and Willison tries it on video](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemini-decisions-api/)

Google has joined Jev and OpenAI in the decision-model category with Gemini Decision 1: you hand it one multimodal input — text, images, audio, video or PDFs — and as many as 1,000 yes/no, multiple-choice or rubric questions, and it scores them in a single forward pass. Google quotes 150-250 ms end-to-end, $0.10 per million input tokens and nothing for output, and says that is nearly five times cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite; those figures are Google's. [Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/gemini-decisions-api/) tried it on his Yonkers mech video and found a difference: where Jev and OpenAI return 1.0 or 0.0, Gemini gave 0.92 that the clip contained a robot in the US, and 0.05 that it had a dragon. Jev remains cheaper, at 4.2 cents per million.

## Agent and AI security

### [llm 0.37.2 stops two ways a third-party endpoint could receive your OpenAI key](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/llm/)

Simon Willison shipped llm 0.37.2 to close a pair of key-leak bugs reported by Daniel de Araujo, both in the path that points LLM at OpenAI-compatible endpoints from other providers. In the first, present since version 0.6, if the named key had never been stored, llm fell back to `OPENAI_API_KEY` and sent that bearer token to the third-party host — the opposite of what the docs promised. A typo in the key name, or a fresh machine with only the OpenAI key exported, was enough. The second, from 0.32a2, hit `responses: true` models: passing `-o chat_completions 1` built a delegate that still thought it needed the OpenAI key and sent it onward. Both are fixed in 0.37.2.

### [Deterministic OS guards beat an LLM judge at protecting a coding harness, by a lot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13960)

Once a coding harness can reach a shell and the network, a prompt injection in retrieved content can turn everyday tools such as bash and curl into the payload. The authors of ActiveSecHarness argue that the defense has to sit in how permissions are composed, rather than in the model. Their design separates intelligence from authority, with capability tokens, a two-dimensional taint lattice, and deterministic Ring-0 guards at the OS boundary. On 252 fixtures, including 12 multi-step kill chains, a local LLM judge refused 98.3% of benign work at 142 ms per call. Ring 0 blocked every attack, let every benign task through, and did it in 12.7 microseconds — about 11,200 times faster. A stateless policy still missed four of the 12 kill chains.

### [Coding agents follow project instruction files more often than not, but the miss rate is all-or-nothing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13709)

Teams increasingly steer coding agents with persistent context files such as `AGENTS.md`, on the assumption that what is written there will actually be done. A study of 621 real snapshots, linked to 6,541 agent-generated commits across 268 repositories, finds that the assumption only half holds. Of 2,053 evaluated instructions, 54.4% were followed in every applicable commit and 37.3% were followed in none, with only 8.3% landing in between — a bimodal split, not a scatter of partial compliance. Commits associated with GitHub Copilot complied more often (66.0% mean) than those from Claude Code (52.2%) or OpenAI Codex (51.2%). A review that samples only the instructions an agent already obeys will miss the third that it never touches.

### [Enterprise agents stay safe when evidence is on the table, and stall less clearly when it is not](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13355)

DEPUTYBENCH asks a more ordinary question than jailbreak suites: can an enterprise agent tell whether a routine request is actually permitted by the organization's own records and policies, and keep doing legitimate work when it is? The benchmark has 79 base items and 375 instances, and two security experts spent two weeks verifying the ground truth. Across 12 models, revised audits give 88.0% pooled safety and 79.1% paired success. The more interesting result is what happens when the evidence goes missing. On 27 matched tasks, agents restricted themselves 10.3% of the time when evidence was absent, against 3.5% when they were shown an explicit non-permission. High safety with the evidence in view, the authors note, does not establish reliable stopping when permission cannot be verified.

### [Geometric access control in RAG isolates documents, but it does not keep embeddings secret](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14061)

Some RAG systems try to enforce access control geometrically: they transform embeddings so that documents under different policies stay retrievable inside their own region of the vector space, without crossing the boundary. A security analysis of the available implementations, across three encoders, finds that this does preserve retrieval quality and provides strong policy isolation — unauthorized documents stay unretrieved — but isolation is not confidentiality. A curious user who holds a few hundred aligned pairs of original and protected embeddings can recover the originals, and can forge policy-compatible queries at a higher cost. Meanwhile, the shape of the transformed space can leak policy membership even when unauthorized retrieval is blocked. Today's geometric schemes, the authors argue, only really deliver the first of these goals.

### [Even lightweight models often evaded commercial endpoint defenses in a new agent benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13684)

Cybersecurity benchmarks already ask whether LLM agents can find vulnerabilities or work through a penetration-testing exercise. BypassGym asks a narrower question: can an agent, given a test payload, produce a program that runs on a real Windows endpoint without a commercial EDR product stopping it? The evaluation runs actual Windows systems and six EDR products inside virtual machines and returns structured detection feedback. The authors tested five lightweight model backends against three payloads and found that even these simple models often succeeded, with DeepSeek-V4.1 Flash doing so in 17 of 18 runs, while Bitdefender stopped all but five of 15. The paper is a measurement for defenders, not a claim that the results will hold against a prepared human adversary.

## Agent memory and context

### [Teaching a coding agent to use ordinary files as memory transfers to Terminal-Bench and SWE-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14009)

Long-horizon agents eventually overflow their context and have to decide what to write down, what to read back, and when. Recent work has tried to teach that control with on-policy distillation, but the memory tools usually sit outside either model's pre-training, so the teacher cannot demonstrate an interface it has never used. CAMP-II's answer is to drop the special tools and use ordinary files instead, in a workspace that survives context replacements. Training one policy across shop, coding, research and visual tasks, with a frozen teacher that already has a prior for file operations, produced 2.97 times the gain of the strongest distillation baseline. The same habit improved success on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-Bench Pro V2, which the trainer never saw.

### [Agents should treat a memory update as a hypothesis, and try to falsify it first](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14326)

Self-improving agents often write a lesson into memory the moment an episode succeeds, which makes local success an unreliable signal for knowledge that will be reused — and can do damage if it is wrong. Falsification-Gated Self-Evolution treats a candidate update as a hypothesis that has to survive attempts to falsify it before it is committed. Across web, app, tool-use and memory benchmarks, the authors report a 39.8% WebArena success rate, 78.8 average completion on AppWorld, and 0.724 / 0.487 Pass@1 on τ-Retail and τ-Airline, while reducing falsification failures to 5.0%, regression damage to 1.3% and harmful committed updates to 3.6%. Those figures are the authors'. The design idea is the one that lingers: test a memory write before letting the agent keep it.

### [Linking each memory lesson back to the trajectory it came from stops some of the harm memory causes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14320)

External memory is supposed to help an agent improve from experience, but it can also make the agent worse: it fails a task it would have solved with a clean slate, because it follows a lesson that is wrong or misapplied. Lessons are usually handed over without the trajectories they came from, so the agent has no way to check them. WikiTrace stores each lesson as a short insight linked to why its trajectory succeeded or failed, and to the raw trajectory itself. On a new task, when an insight lacks a needed detail or came from a failed task, the agent walks those links to correct it before acting. On five agentic benchmarks, WikiTrace beat existing memory methods and reduced this memory-induced degradation.

### [A short burst of extra agent sessions can leave a prefix-cached server half-dead for an hour](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13412)

Serving LLM agents cheaply depends on prefix caching: a hit means prefilling about a thousand new tokens, a miss means recomputing a 10-40K-token context. This paper shows that the arrangement is metastable. With an identical base workload, a few minutes of extra arrivals left a vLLM server in a degraded regime — roughly a 10% cache-hit ratio, about half the throughput, and a backlog that kept growing for the remaining 70 minutes — while a twin that never saw the burst stayed healthy. Throttling new admissions recovered only one of seven collapsed runs, while suspending already admitted sessions recovered all of them. A budgeter that caps the resident KV demand of active sessions prevented the failure in every run the authors tested.

### [Harness memory does not teach an agent new kinds of tasks; updating the weights can](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14505)

Continual-ARC is a lifelong stream built from the three ARC-AGI editions, designed so that memory-based harnesses and weight-based learners can be scored on the same scale. Tasks return after dormant gaps with fresh instances, and new ones keep arriving. After the stream, the learner is frozen and tested on fresh instances of tasks it has already met, and on unseen tasks. Running a frozen-weight agent harness on Qwen3.5-9B and GLM-5.3 Flash, the authors find that persistence generally does not improve memorization unless the harness is given explicit expert guidance, and that most harness methods still learn less than a floor that simply caches each task's demonstrations. No harness-based method gained on unseen tasks, whereas weight-based learning showed substantial gains there.

## Harnesses and coding agents

### [AWS shipped a local emulator that agents built, and Swami says the engineer's job is the guardrails](https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2026/11/aws-emulator-and-the-engineers-job-when-agents-write-the-code.html)

AWS Emulator is an official, free, offline copy of AWS for tests and coding agents, covering more than 200 services and released to GitHub under a CC0 public-domain dedication. [Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/aws-emulator/) notes that it is written in Rust, accumulated 29,684 commits in the first three weeks, and currently needs Docker on macOS and Windows. The more interesting story is how it was built. Swaminathan Sivasubramanian, writing on Werner Vogels' blog, says all of the emulator's code was written and tested by agents, checked against real AWS with fidelity tests, and passed through a merge gate that includes security agents doing adversarial review. His lesson is that the engineer's job is now those guardrails. The "agents wrote all the code" claim is AWS's.

### [FlowPlan cuts a coding agent's token bill by planning at a higher level, without hurting resolve rate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13241)

Claude Code and its peers can resolve real repository issues, but the iterative loop — read, edit, re-read, re-test — is expensive. FlowPlan asks whether the agent can reason about a repair at an abstract program level, rather than repeatedly operating over raw source, and verify the result explicitly instead of re-reading the tree. Across six benchmark-and-model settings it did not significantly change the resolution rate, and it reduced tokens in every setting, significantly in five of six. With Claude Haiku 4.5 it used only 0.46-0.47× the baseline tokens on SWE-bench Pro and 0.32-0.35× on SWE-bench Verified; with Sonnet 5 the savings were smaller, 0.72-0.94× depending on the bench. It posted the largest token cut among the compared methods in three of the six settings.

### [A teacher that models the student writes better skills, and the library still helps after the teacher leaves](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13144)

Teaching is not only about the task; it is also about the learner. This paper asks whether a stronger model teaches a fixed student better when it builds a model of that student, tests hypotheses about its limitations with diagnostic probes, and only then writes reusable skills for the failures it has actually diagnosed. With Claude Sonnet 5 teaching DeepSeek-V4-Flash, that student-aware transfer reached 86.46% task success across four runs, ahead of a profile-free teacher using the same framework (81.04%) and of the untaught student (75.00%) in every run. Removing the analytical learner model hurt both prediction and success, and under misleading profiles none of 48 candidate skills passed admission. Saved libraries kept average gains of up to 11.1 points after the teacher had left.

### [ReTrace distills a tool-using agent into a planner-free workflow that is almost as accurate and much faster](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14492)

An LLM agent can follow a standard operating procedure written in English, but it calls the model to choose every step of every run, which is slow and expensive at scale. ReTrace distills those runs into a planner-free workflow: a fixed path of tool calls, with the model used only where the original agent actually needed it. On 79 procedures from the authors' production platform, the workflows reached 93.8% completion against the agent's 96.8%, at 24% lower cost. On τ²-Bench they reached 75.8% task success against the agent's 80.6%, and finished in 1.3 seconds instead of 170. On eight SOP-Bench procedures, repair took ReTrace to 87.8% at 0.40 seconds per task, against the agent's 85.5% in 46.6 seconds.

### [A single agent is usually the efficient default; multi-agent only pays off when the implementation is good](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12953)

Enterprises are putting LLM agents on long, branching standard operating procedures, but there is little controlled evidence for choosing among a single agent, a skill-augmented one, or a multi-agent setup. Holding the model, tasks and tools fixed and varying only the architecture, this study compares four designs across 13 enterprise SOPs. There is no universal winner, and no architecture was statistically separable in aggregate. Every coordinated arm spent 1.4-4.7× the tokens and 2.6-5.8× the latency of the single agent, which makes the single agent the efficient default. More importantly, implementation quality outweighed the architectural choice: a plausible multi-agent design lost to the single agent, but a targeted redesign of it became the only arm to beat the baseline significantly, 75.6% versus 67.4%.

### [Frontier coding agents still leave most expert tickets unfinished, even the ones at the top of the board](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13246)

ExpertCrowd-SWE is a benchmark of 100 expert-authored, multi-file engineering tasks in large, unfamiliar codebases: each is a change request written by a practising engineer against a pinned commit, with a grader that awards partial credit and encodes what a maintainer would refuse to merge. The tasks span 87 open-source projects and were selected for difficulty against current frontier models. Seven models, five runs each: Claude Opus 5.5 (67.3%) and GPT-6 Astra (63.7%) lead without a statistically significant gap, then Claude Fable 5.1 (46.5%) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (40.0%), with Qwen 3.8 Max, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Grok 4.6 bunched at 32-34%. No task is fully solved by every model, and on 80 of the 100 no model earns full credit on all five runs.

### [In failed long-horizon runs, the evidence of the error is often already in the trajectory — and unused](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13254)

When an LLM agent fails part-way through a long task, the usual write-up records a symptom or the step that looks responsible. It does not say where the error entered, or whether the trajectory already contradicted it. The authors annotated 95 failed runs of six multi-agent systems. Faults enter early — the median is 18% of the way through — and in 37 of 88 Who&When runs they enter before the step human labels mark as decisive. In 72% of failed runs the trajectory already held evidence against the error; in 88% of those, no agent acted on it. A further 45% of the Who&When failures ended with a content-filter crash, after which the harness emitted a final answer no agent had produced.

### [Veritas writes the verifiers that self-improving agents usually have to be given by hand](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13336)

A self-improving system is only as good as the verifier that judges each round, and those verifiers are almost always written by hand, which does not scale to tasks that arrive without one. Veritas is a harness built specifically to generate them. Unlike a general-purpose agent harness, it adds an evidence header that states what the verifier will observe, a library of domain rules that tasks leave unstated, and a set of authoring principles, some of them refined by a self-improvement loop. With the model held fixed, Veritas's verifiers agree better with official verdicts than those of seven other harnesses on all four execution-scored benchmarks, and they are at or near the best on two pairwise ones.

### [Skills evolve more cleanly when the procedure is a program you can patch, not a paragraph you rewrite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14019)

Agent skills have become a popular way to give an LLM reusable knowledge without touching its weights, and recent work goes further by distilling experience from trajectories and evolving those skills from task feedback. The usual representation, though, is a natural-language document, which intertwines procedural logic with descriptive knowledge and makes a targeted update painful. PASE splits the two: reusable procedures become editable programs, so a failed step can be patched in the executable part without rewriting the surrounding prose. The authors say this consistently outperforms their baselines, with gains of just over 2.8%, and that the evolved skills are more stable and generalize better — a modest lift, honestly reported, whose interest is the representation rather than the leaderboard.

### [Claude Code graded handwritten exams in close agreement with a lecturer, and still stumbled on diagrams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13217)

A computer-graphics course asked whether Claude Code, running Claude Opus 4.6 as an agent guided by three rubric files, could grade handwritten mid-term papers reliably enough to help an instructor. Sixty consenting students' 20-mark scripts were graded in a single agentic session. On a 20-paper reliability sample, agreement with expert human totals was strong (r = 0.97, mean absolute error 0.85 marks), with a small but significant AI-positive bias of 0.68 marks. Three failure modes kept recurring: keyword matching that missed conceptual depth, confusion about corrected multiple-choice selections, and attention drifting off hand-drawn diagrams. The authors conclude that AI grading can assist on objective items under safeguards, but is not ready to replace instructor judgment at the boundaries.

### [Grounding a static-analysis finding in the code makes a fix more likely, but it does not buy trust](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13041)

Developers routinely dismiss static-analysis findings they cannot interpret. An LLM can supply the missing explanation, but designs that also let the model do the detection give up the auditability that makes static analysis defensible. This work keeps the detector's verdict untouched and asks the agent only to explain it, grounded in the code. A within-subjects experiment with 24 people compared the redesign with its predecessor. Perceived usability rose from 77.3 to 85.0 on the System Usability Scale, actionability improved most, and the odds of a correct remediation were 2.6 times higher. Trust was the one measure that did not move. Closing interviews suggested that grounding made findings easier to understand rather than easier to believe.

## Evaluation and reasoning

### [Agent benchmarks stop ranking the best models long before they run out of tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13541)

Most agent benchmarks collapse a run to one pass/fail bit, and this paper argues that the bit fails in two ways. It is too coarse: neighbouring top-ten agents on SWE-bench Verified differ by less than the tests' own errors, and correcting those errors reorders neighbours no more than deleting a random sixth of each agent's passes. It is also capped at the reference solution. ALE-Bench's top ten, who separate cleanly when scored by contest points, all tie when graded against the median human, even though nearly half the runs beat that human. In a Terminal-Bench re-run, models that tied on pass/fail differed more than tenfold on the quantity the tests had been discarding.

### [Trace-based evaluation and security red-teaming catch different agent failures, with no overlap in one study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14493)

Deploying an analytics agent in an enterprise means being able to detect failures across multi-tool workflows, and this paper compares three evaluation frameworks in three phases. Repeated measurements find that 97% of configurations meet a stability threshold, and that the pattern does not depend on the agent. On 72 ground-truth cases, the trace-based framework had the best precision-recall balance (F1 = 0.90), while a text-only judge matching it on recall produced five times more false alarms. The more surprising result is on unknown risks: the study uncovered 24 novel failures with zero overlap between functional testing and security red-teaming. The authors conclude that functional and security evaluation are complementary, and suggest using both rather than picking one.

## Policy

### [The AI 2027 authors have moved their "superhuman researcher" date back to 2027](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/q35-timelines-update-looks-like-the)

Responding to METR's early-November assessment of Anthropic's AI-accelerated research, already covered here on 6 November, the six authors of the AI 2027 forecast have shortened their timelines again. METR estimated that Anthropic is now making progress about twice as fast as its historical rate, and guessed that "perhaps ≈80%" of that acceleration is due to AI. The project treats that as a 1.7× AI-attributable speedup, a pace even its most aggressive model had given less than a 4% chance of seeing by this November. All six now forecast that a Superhuman AI Researcher will likely arrive sometime in 2027, assuming no substantial slowdown, and they put a 35-65% chance on AI that automates all white-collar work arriving before 2028. These are forecasts, not measurements.
