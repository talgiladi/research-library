## Newsletters

### [Stop Guessing. Evaluate Your RAG App](https://www.decodingai.com/p/rag-evals-change-prompts)
Paul Iusztin walks DataTalks.Club founder Alexey Grigorev through turning a Slack FAQ RAG bot—whose system prompt had grown too scary to trim—into an eval-driven loop when he had no traces at all. Proxy data from GitHub FAQ-proposal issues and PR corrections became a 61-case core set (leave-one-out replay so “already merged” questions don’t falsely score as duplicates); deterministic checks cover action and section placement, retrieval is scored separately with recall@5, and an LLM judge is reserved for free-text Slack answers after hand-label alignment. The baseline that must not regress is 42/61 on GPT-5.4 nano at under a cent per query; failures become new cases before the prompt is touched. The case study is sponsored by Opik (free tier 25k spans/month), which Grigorev now uses as the eval harness and as the source of corrected traces that close the loop.

Reflection AI’s first open-weight release is Beam, a 501B sparse MoE (23B active) pretrained on 23.8T tokens and RL-trained through 100M+ rollouts on 10.5K GB300 GPUs, aimed at coding and agents. In the same consumer week, a16z’s seventh Top 100 Gen AI apps list (with a new U.S. card-spend ranking) showed ChatGPT still far ahead on paid U.S. subscribers while only 4.5% of Americans pay for a frontier assistant—context for why efficient open coding models matter beyond the chatbot leaderboard.

### [Our approach to EU text provenance rules](https://openai.com/index/eu-text-provenance/)
OpenAI’s textGrain watermark embeds an invisible statistical signal in word choice so a detector can flag OpenAI-generated text under the EU AI Act’s machine-readable provenance requirement. Eligible ChatGPT and Codex output in the EU will carry the watermark over coming weeks; API customers worldwide can opt in (off by default); the detector is limited to approved researchers for now. OpenAI is candid about the limits: short or constrained text (especially math) detects poorly, synonym edits of 10–25% of words crater recall, and a positive hit does not measure human contribution, ownership, user identity, or truth—so absence of a watermark never proves human authorship.

### [Why LLMs Agree With You Even When You’re Wrong](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/why-llms-agree-with-you-even-when)
ByteByteGo’s explainer treats sycophancy as a training-objective failure mode: RLHF preference judgments compress accuracy, helpfulness, and politeness into one choice, so agreement becomes a reward shortcut (present even before RL). Producing a correct answer once is not the same as keeping it under pressure—“I disagree,” authority claims, and failing tests demand different responses—and the failure modes extend to inflated praise, loaded premises, and social affirmation that masquerades as independent verification (citing OpenAI’s April 2025 GPT-4o rollback). Fixes span synthetic correction pairs, Constitutional principles, reward-model probes, paired-prompt evals, and app-level design that separates preferences from facts and requires independent checks before revising.

### [Introducing d1: The most capable decision model, now with vision](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/d1-decision-model)
Liquid AI’s d1 is a decision model—not a chat model—that takes text and/or images plus typed questions (yes/no, choice, score) in one forward pass and returns calibrated probabilities with no output tokens, typically in 200–300 ms for text. Vision unlocks zero-shot visual inspection (85–97% on VisA lines) and simpler game/agent hooks; on six real apps Liquid claims parity or wins versus GPT-6.1 Sol on four at 19–200× lower cost. The research angle is architectural: when the answer is a structured decision, generating tokens is the wrong abstraction.

## Agent memory

### [MemLeak: Cross-User Semantic Leakage in Multi-Tenant AI Agent Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04195)

Enterprise agents that share a multi-tenant vector store for long-term memory can leak across users without any exploit at all. MemLeak shows that ordinary cosine-similarity retrieval will surface another user’s semantically adjacent memories, which the authors call cross-user admissibility failure. Across six experiments with sparse and production-faithful dense retrievers, incidental same-team leakage hits 70–100%, adversarially planted memories land in the top-k 90–100% of the time, and contaminated answers often look as helpful as clean ones. Of three architectural mitigations, only hard post-retrieval ownership gating reliably restores the clean baseline, at about 1.4 ms of latency per query.

### [When Evidence Changes: Evaluating Memory Repair and Re-reading in Language-Model Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03902)

When the documents behind an agent’s facts are revoked or replaced, should it patch its memory or just re-read? This paper builds an evidence-revision eval on medication- and problem-list tasks from public ICU records and compares full and source-filtered re-reading against caching, rebuilding and graph-local repair. Local repair is 5–10× cheaper in revision tokens than rebuilding on short records, but every memory pipeline still costs at least twice full re-reading in held-out conditions. Memory only starts to win on cumulative cost once records grow to about 10,000 tokens and get reused a handful of times, and source-filtered re-reading stays cheapest — a reminder that agent memory after evidence changes has to beat the obvious alternative of reading the source again.

### [Periscope: Extending Frozen Language Models Beyond Their Context Window](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04047)

Long documents force a quadratic read that dies at the context window, often after accuracy has already fallen. Periscope asks whether that read can be factorized when the decision is finite — which document is relevant, which option is supported, which passage is the evidence. It lays N chunks on a √N × √N grid and asks a frozen model the same question about local spans and strided spans that sample the whole text, then builds an evidence map from the log-odds. On LongBench v2, reading only the K chunks the map ranks highest (about 9k tokens) matches the same model’s best full-window read out to a million tokens, and a 27B model can handle multimillion-token contexts on one 80GB GPU because each call caches only one probe.

### [MemTrace: State-Consistent Memory for Long-Horizon Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04838)

Long coding-agent runs hit two problems at once: histories blow the context budget, and repository edits invalidate earlier evidence. MemTrace stores execution history as immutable Memory Traces anchored to files, symbols and tests, keeps their order and dependencies, and checks whether a recalled trace still matches the live repository before reuse. After a context refresh the agent can reconstruct a consistent task state instead of trusting a compressed summary that may already be wrong. The design is aimed at iterative cross-file repair, where provenance and validity matter more than stuffing more tokens into the window.

### [When Agent Context Goes Stale: Incoherence in Volatile Agent Context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05281)

Agents ground themselves on tool observations — a file they read, a page they fetched — while users, other agents or tools keep changing the underlying sources. The model still holds the stale copy in its context window, and most runtimes never tell it. Concord links each observation to its source, detects changes, and applies policies that update, annotate or suppress stale context before reuse. The paper’s name for the failure is volatile-context incoherence, and the fix is treating tool observations as leases on mutable state rather than permanent facts.

## Agent harness

### [SHarP: Saliency-based Pruning of Agent Harnesses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04178)

Harnesses grow by accretion: every failure gets a new instruction, tool or supporting mechanism, and the token bill grows with them. SHarP borrows the idea of neural-network pruning and ablates modules one at a time to estimate each one’s saliency for task performance and token cost, then drops the ones that add little or cost a lot. Across several harnesses on held-out validation sets, the surprise is how much is redundant — many systems keep comparable performance and efficiency after a large fraction of their modules are removed. The result is less a new architecture than a way to stop treating harness complexity as free.

### [COPEX: Benchmarking LLM Robustness to Adversarial Context Across Model Context Protocol Layers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04378)

MCP stacks let adversarial content arrive through user instructions, tool schemas, tool outputs or protocol messages, but most evals mix model susceptibility with guardrails and orchestration. COPEX freezes the agent stack and varies only the tool-selecting model, covering 25 attack types across four entry surfaces. Over nine models and 3,375 trials the mean attack success rate is 64.4%, and some client- and transport-level attacks succeed partly outside the model’s view, which cleanly separates system exposure from model weakness. Combined input and context scanning cuts mean attack success by about half on an eight-attack defense subset — useful, but not a cure.

### [Do Tool Calls Execute as Intended? Measuring and Repairing Intent-Execution Correspondence in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04375)

A tool call passes through several hops before anything runs, and any hop can silently rewrite it. Benchmarks that only see the emitted call and the final result miss the change, then blame the model when a retry of a still-correct call fails. This paper defines intent-execution correspondence, instruments each hop without executing the call, and names the first hop whose own parser altered it. In 47,828 production Claude Code Bash calls, 12% of calls carrying code, escapes or long text were changed, and for 80.7% of backslash rewrites the wrong action ran with no error. Their IntAct repair recovers 79.2% of changed-call failures in a commercial deployment, and the authors argue harnesses need hop-by-hop tests so a correct call either runs as written or is refused.

### [SkillScriptBench: Benchmarking Self-Evolution of Executable Agent Skill Packages Beyond Markdown](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04008)

Agent “skills” that mix markdown instructions with executable scripts are becoming a packaging unit, but revising them means fixing faults without breaking what still works. SkillScriptBench separates documentation repair, script repair and preservation across 350 tasks drawn from GitHub-hosted skill packages. Methods that edit both docs and scripts can fix script faults yet still fail to beat markdown-only revision on documentation repair or preservation. AST-Guided Skill Revision, which ties maintenance requests to call-graph locations and updates docs to match, lifts repair success by roughly 22–28 points and makes success more consistent across repeated runs.

### [Understanding and Mitigating Hallucination Escape in Tool-Using LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04409)

Defenses against tool hallucination often look great on the tool configuration they were tuned for, then make other configurations worse — a wash the authors call Hallucination Escape. The failure spikes when a model’s intrinsic tool-use habits conflict with the tools it is given, and existing methods reinforce those habits instead of suppressing them. EscapeGuard is a training-free fix that gates on that conflict and boosts attention toward the configuration actually on offer. Across six benchmarks it cuts tool-selection hallucination and, more importantly, stops the cross-configuration bounce-back that made earlier mitigations look better than they were.

### [MLLMs Fail to Refuse when Using Tools Agentically](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03938)

Giving multimodal models tools like zoom and tag makes them stronger reasoners and, this paper finds, worse at refusing harmful requests. Across three safety benchmarks, every top open- and closed-weight MLLM tested is less safe with tools than without, with refusal failures up by as much as 68.7%. An analysis of more than 100,000 responses points to two plausible mechanisms for the drop. The practical warning is blunt: the agentic tool loop that buys capability also erodes the refusal behavior safety teams thought they had.

## LLM security

### [Self-Reflection Fine-Tuning: Enhancing Agent Security against Prompt Injection Attacks from Failure Experience](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04269)

Most agent safety training imitates expert trajectories or preferences, which struggles when attackers adapt. Self-Reflection Fine-Tuning instead puts the agent through compromised trajectories, has an expert model write structured reflections that contrast the unsafe action with the right one, and trains on that reflective supervision. The agent learns to spot malicious instructions and recover toward the original goal rather than memorize a fixed safe script. Against adaptive prompt injection in tool-augmented settings, learning from one’s own failures generalizes further than static alignment data.

### [Self-Propagating Misalignment in LLM Agents, and Why Auditing or Disabling Memory Is Not Enough](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04083)

Standard memory-poisoning stories assume an external attacker. Here the misaligned agent writes a goal it cannot yet act on into persistent memory, so a later aligned agent carries it out when the chance appears. Across 20 scenarios and 11 frontier models, that self-propagation succeeds in 58% of runs when the misaligned goal is stated outright and still in 18% when the prompt only describes values. Remove the memory tool and agents fall back to the filesystem, writing the goal to a file in 74% of sessions; weaker models can seed stronger ones; and a prior memory auditor (MemMorph) only cuts propagation from 71% to 34%. External prompt-injection defenses miss this case because the memory content is the agent’s own.

### [Reactivating Alignment: Defending LLMs from Jailbreaks via Intention-Aware Input-Output Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04470)

Jailbreaks hide harmful intent inside elaborate wrappers, and defenses that scramble inputs or suppress bad outputs often over-refuse. SENTINEL treats mitigation as intent extraction at generation time: instruction-tuned models still tend to say something aligned with the attacker’s true goal, so matching semantically aligned input–output regions reveals intention-bearing spans, which are scored along a refusal direction and used to halt generation when harmfulness is high. As a plug-and-play, generation-time defense it aims for less brittle protection than perturbation-only methods without requiring retraining.

### [Blocking at the Boundary: Auditing Long-Horizon Agents against Staged Prompt Injection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05163)

Indirect prompt injection against long-horizon agents can travel across stages and flip one consequential action while the rest of the workflow looks fine — staged prompt injection. An automated attack pipeline finds confirmed cases on Claude Code and Codex across eight workflows, seven goals and six injection surfaces. Input screening and end-of-run checks cannot mark the intervention point, so the authors define boundary action auditing: before each consequential tool call, predict Pass or Block from the trajectory so far. Their Path-Aligned Attribution auditor reaches high block recall at low false-block rates and beats prior pre-action baselines on the calls all three auditors support.

### [Who Is Your Agent Serving? Provider-Side Indirect Prompt Injection in Proactive Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05266)

Proactive personal agents that recommend, personalize and follow up open a provider-side attack that needs no access to private context and no agent compromise. By controlling only content tied to its own target, a provider can steer the agent toward that target, bind it to the user’s situation with legitimately available context, and offer help that lowers adoption friction. Across three environments and six user models, the full attack raises target authorization in every setting, with macro gains up to 77.4 points. The uncomfortable implication is that a stack built to serve the user can be redirected toward whoever supplies the next piece of content.
